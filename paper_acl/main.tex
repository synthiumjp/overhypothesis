\title{A Noun's Training History Decides Whether Small Transformers\\Take Its Features from Context}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Small transformers trained on text in which nearby sentences describe the same kind learn to carry a feature from one mention of a noun to the next. Probes of this use nonce nouns, which are both unfamiliar and without stored knowledge. In two registered experiments with 150 models (3.4M to 25.6M parameters, ten seeds) trained on an artificial language, we controlled exactly how often and in what arrangement nonce nouns appeared. When a noun's sentences came one at a time, carry of a shape given in the context fell from 4.5 nats for unseen nouns to under 1 nat by 100 exposures, whether the noun had been seen with a fixed shape, a changing shape or no shape. When the same number of sentences came in runs of four that shared a shape, with the shape changing between runs, familiar nouns kept 3.4 nats more carry, also at 1,000 exposures. Runs without a shared shape helped only at 100 exposures, and a stored shape lowered carry by a further 1.0 nat. In models trained with stable shapes, rescaling or replacing the input rows of unseen nouns showed that their carry depends mostly on where a row points, not on its size. In pretrained Pythia models (1B to 12B), real nouns took 1.9 to 2.7 nats of colour from the context; neither replacing their input rows nor how typical their colour was changed this reliably. In the small models, a familiar noun kept taking a feature from the context only when that noun's own contexts had made doing so useful.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A language model can take a word's properties from the text around it or from what it has stored about the word. Which it does depends on its training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning in context; common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights \citep{chan2022,chan2025}. In pretrained models, heads that copy from the context compete with recall from memory \citep{yu2023,jin2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask about single words: as a model sees a noun more often, when does it stop taking the noun's features from the context, and what about the noun's training decides this?

Probes with nonce words cannot answer this, because a nonce word is new in two ways. It has no stored knowledge, and it also has an unfamiliar representation, and conclusions drawn with nonce words can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}. We met this problem in our own earlier experiments (Section~\ref{sec:background}). Small transformers trained on grouped text carried a shape from one mention of an unseen noun to the next, but most of that carry vanished when the same noun was given an input embedding typical of trained nouns.

We therefore controlled, for each of 40 nonce nouns, how many times it appeared in training, with what shapes, and how its sentences were arranged. Two experiments were registered before any of their corpora was built (Appendices~\ref{app:M} and~\ref{app:N}). The second was designed to test two confounds that a blind review found in the first. We find:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Familiar nouns stopped carrying when their sentences came singly.} Carry fell from 4.5 nats for unseen nouns to under 1 nat by 100 exposures, whatever the noun had been seen with (Experiment~1).
\item \textbf{Nouns whose own contexts were useful kept carrying.} With sentences in runs of four that shared a shape, and the shape changing between runs, carry stayed 3.4 nats higher. Runs alone helped briefly, and a stored shape lowered carry (Experiment~2).
\item \textbf{Where the input row points matters more than its size.} Rescaling unseen nouns' rows to the trained nouns' norm lowered carry by 0.5 nats; the trained nouns' mean row removed it (Experiment~3).
\item \textbf{In pretrained models we found no such link.} In Pythia, real nouns carried colour from the context whatever their input rows and whether or not their colour was typical (Experiment~4).
\end{enumerate}
Our claims are about transformers, not children.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}. It favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023}, and its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare and unseen tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies the most frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. In this work how familiar an item is goes together with whether a stored answer exists; we vary familiarity, stored knowledge and arrangement separately for single words within one model.

\textbf{Context against memory.} Pretrained models answer from memory more for frequent entities and need retrieval for rare ones \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023}; real entities are harder to move by context than invented ones \citep{du2024}. Heads that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts \citep{yu2023,jin2024}. Here frequency is observed, not manipulated.

\textbf{Nonce words and exposure.} Nonce words are the usual test of generalisation to a new item \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and controlling how often an item occurs shows how frequency shapes what transformers learn \citep{wei2021}. We control exposure down to the window in which each occurrence is read.

\section{Setting and earlier results}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by a nonce noun, with 14 exemplars per kind. Each exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten nonce words, and is described in one of several frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture; Appendix~\ref{app:I}). In \textbf{Regular} corpora each kind keeps one shape. In \textbf{Reassigned} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass through the corpus and keeps it within the pass, so only the context can tell a trained noun's current shape. In \textbf{grouped} order, sentences about a kind come in runs of four; in \textbf{random} order they are shuffled (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). Text is read once, in non-overlapping 128-token windows. Models are decoder-only transformers of 3.4M, 11.0M and 25.6M parameters (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). Every artificial word is one token.

\textbf{Carry.} A test item gives one context sentence about a noun with shape $s$, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a $s$ \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}, then \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the log probability $L$ of each of the ten shapes at the next position. \textbf{Carry} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes (excluding the noun's own trained shape $s^*$, if it has one). \textbf{Knowledge} is $L(s^*)$ minus the mean over the other nine shapes after an unrelated sentence.

\textbf{Earlier results.} In registered experiments with 120 models (Appendices~\ref{app:A} and~\ref{app:E}), grouped training raised a related index of carry for unseen nouns over random order by 11.08 nats [9.57,~12.59]. But the unseen nouns' input embeddings had never been trained (an intended tie with the output embeddings was lost), and when the same nouns were given an embedding typical of trained nouns, the effect shrank to 3.00 nats [1.79,~4.21] (exploratory; Appendix~\ref{app:L}). So the earlier probes measured arrangement together with the test noun's representation. In the experiments below, input and output embeddings are untied by design.

\section{Experiment 1: exposure and history}\label{sec:exp1}
\textbf{Design.} We added 40 nonce nouns to three base corpora (Regular grouped, Regular random and Reassigned grouped). In every model they fill 13 cells: three histories, each at 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, three nouns per cell, plus four unseen nouns. A \emph{fixed-shape} noun always appears with its own shape $s^*$; an \emph{uninformative} noun with a new shape every sentence, drawn from the corpus's shapes; a \emph{shape-absent} noun only in a frame that names no shape (\emph{The \textit{noun} is \textit{colour} and \textit{texture}}, which trained kinds also use). For comparison, a trained kind appears in about 60,000 sentences. Each familiarity sentence lies wholly inside one window, at most one per window, between runs in grouped corpora; a noun's exposures are evenly spaced from 5\% to 95\% of training. A checker verified every exposure from the tokens, and the corpora matched checksums recorded before training. We trained 90 models (three sizes, ten seeds, three base corpora). Unseen nouns are also scored with an input row typical of trained nouns (the trained nouns' mean plus a random offset of typical length).

\textbf{Analysis.} The seed is the unit of replication: we average cell means over the three sizes within each seed and test over ten seeds, with \citet{holm1979} correction within each experiment. A hypothesis is supported only if its direction also holds at two of the three sizes. Hypotheses are numbered as in the registrations.

\textbf{Results.} Fixed-shape nouns learned their shape (knowledge at 1,000 exposures 8.3 to 9.5 nats in the three corpora; M1 passed). In grouped Regular models, carry was 4.53 nats [3.54,~5.52] for unseen nouns and had largely gone by 100 exposures for every history (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left): 3.6 to 4.5 nats at 10 exposures, 0.16 to 0.86 at 100, and within 0.4 nats of zero from 1,000 on. Grouped models carried more than random ones for unseen nouns (4.68 [3.71,~5.65]), and in random order carry was near zero for every noun.

We predicted that carry would fall faster for fixed-shape than for shape-absent nouns (H30). It did not (slope difference 0.16 nats per decade [0.01,~0.30], the wrong way), although fixed-shape nouns carried 0.3 to 0.8 nats less throughout (exploratory). Their stored preference for $s^*$ rose by 2.97 nats per decade (H32). Familiar shape-absent nouns at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures carried 0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52] more than unseen nouns with a typical row; this difference was reliable, and the two were also equivalent within the registered 1-nat bound (H31). In Reassigned models (exploratory), familiar nouns kept more carry (shape-absent: about 2 nats at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures) and their history mattered more (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, middle).

A registered search for heads did not find the carry. Ablating the heads with the highest induction-head signature \citep{olsson2022} removed a mean proportion of 0.22 [0.07,~0.36] of unseen carry, against a registered 0.5, and not reliably more than random heads (H33); in Reassigned models it cut trained kinds' carry by 7.41 nats, but random heads cut it by 6.91 (H34; Appendix~\ref{app:M}).

\textbf{Two confounds.} A blind review noted two differences between familiar nouns and the trained kinds that might, rather than familiarity, explain the loss of carry. First, a familiar noun never recurred within a window, while the trained kinds came in runs, where an earlier sentence predicts the next. Second, shape-absent nouns never appeared in the test frame. Experiment~2 tests both.

\section{Experiment 2: recurrence and useful context}\label{sec:exp2}
\textbf{Design.} The 40 nonce nouns were reassigned to 12 cells, three nouns each, plus four unseen nouns: three histories $\times$ two presentations $\times$ 100 or 1,000 exposures (Table~\ref{tab:design2}). Presented \emph{singly}, a noun has one sentence per window. Presented in \emph{runs}, it has four consecutive sentences in one window. Exposures count sentences, so runs and singles are matched in sentences, though not in the number or spacing of occasions. Shape-absent nouns now use a frame that begins like the test frame but names no shape (\emph{A \textit{noun} is a \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}), which trained kinds also use in one sentence in fifteen. Everything else is as in Experiment~1. We trained 60 models on Regular grouped (confirmatory) and Reassigned grouped (exploratory) corpora, with three sizes and ten seeds.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} We registered: runs and singles differ for shape-absent nouns (H40) and fixed nouns (H41), each two-sided with equivalence bounds of $\pm$0.3 nats; rotating runs carry more than rotating singles (H42); fixed singles carry less than shape-absent singles (H43); shape-absent singles differ from unseen nouns with a typical row (H44, two-sided, $\pm$0.3). A check (K1) required fixed nouns' knowledge to be equivalent within $\pm$1 nat between runs and singles; otherwise H41 could reflect a change in what is stored rather than recurrence. The 100- and 1,000-exposure cells are pooled.

\textbf{A useful context kept carry.} Unseen nouns carried 6.35 nats [5.59,~7.12] (M2 passed). Rotating nouns in runs carried 3.39 nats [2.69,~4.09] more than rotating nouns presented singly (H42 supported; Figure~\ref{fig:a10}), and the gap was as large at 1,000 exposures (3.35) as at 100 (3.43). At 1,000 exposures rotating runs still carried 3.85 nats, against 0.50 to 1.03 for every other familiar cell (Table~\ref{tab:N2}). Rotating singles are Experiment~1's uninformative history, and they lost carry as before.

\textbf{Recurrence alone helped only briefly.} Shape-absent nouns in runs carried 0.80 nats [0.47,~1.13] more than in singles (H40, ``above''), but the difference was 1.55 at 100 exposures and 0.05 [\textminus{}0.30,~0.40] at 1,000. Fixed nouns in runs also carried more (H41, 1.69 [1.29,~2.08]), but K1 failed: at 100 exposures runs weakened the stored shape (knowledge 3.0 against 5.4 nats; Table~\ref{tab:N3}). By the registered rule we do not read H41 as a recurrence effect.

\textbf{A stored shape lowered carry.} With frames matched, fixed singles carried 1.02 nats [0.76,~1.27] less than shape-absent singles (H43 supported; 1.57 at 100 exposures, 0.46 at 1,000). Shape-absent singles carried 1.42 nats [1.12,~1.73] more than unseen nouns with a typical row (H44, ``above'', not equivalent). This difference was 2.48 at 100 exposures and 0.37 [0.15,~0.59] at 1,000, close to the 0.33 of Experiment~1 at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures.

\textbf{Frame and level.} Shape-absent nouns, trained in a frame where a colour follows \emph{is a}, expected a colour rather than a shape at the test position, whereas every other familiar noun expected a shape (Table~\ref{tab:N3}). Their carry is thus measured where they expect another word class. This does not touch H42, where both conditions expect a shape. Carry was higher overall than in Experiment~1 (unseen 6.35 against 4.53 nats; familiar nouns at 100 exposures 1.5 to 3.1 against 0.2 to 0.9). The experiments differ in frames and in the other nouns in the corpus, so we compare conditions only within an experiment.

\textbf{Reassigned models (exploratory).} The same pattern was larger: runs minus singles was 1.71 nats for shape-absent and 4.67 for rotating nouns, and rotating runs at 1,000 exposures carried more than unseen nouns (6.82 against 5.02). Here a stored shape did not lower carry (fixed minus shape-absent singles 0.11 [\textminus{}0.07,~0.29]; Table~\ref{tab:N4}).

\section{Experiment 3: what about the row matters}\label{sec:exp3}
A noun enters the model only through its input row, so in Experiment~1's 30 grouped Regular models (and, exploratory, the 30 Reassigned ones) we changed only the rows of unseen nouns and scored carry again; unchanged rows reproduced the original scores. Scaling an unseen noun's row to the trained kinds' mean norm, keeping its direction, lowered carry from 4.53 to 4.00 nats (difference 0.52 [0.20,~0.85]; H49 supported, at two of three sizes). A random direction at that norm gave the same carry (4.09), and the trained kinds' mean row removed it (\textminus{}0.44). Shrinking a familiar shape-absent noun's row (10,000 exposures) to the unseen nouns' norm raised its carry only from \textminus{}0.05 to 0.53. So in grouped Regular models the size of a row explains a small part of the carry of unseen nouns, and most of it depends on where the row points. In Reassigned models (exploratory) rescaling raised unseen carry (by 0.27), and shrinking a familiar row raised its carry from 1.86 to 4.16 (Table~\ref{tab:N5}).

\section{Experiment 4: pretrained models}\label{sec:exp4}
\textbf{Design.} Do pretrained models show a link between a real noun's input row and its carry, or carry less for nouns with a typical colour? We used Pythia deduplicated models \citep{biderman2023}, final checkpoints, with 1B, 1.4B, 2.8B, 6.9B and 12B parameters as primary models (70M and 160M descriptive; a 410M pilot set the equivalence bound). Forty-four one-token nouns formed 22 pairs, each a colour-typical noun (e.g., \emph{sky}, \emph{leaf}, \emph{bone}) and a colour-variable noun (e.g., \emph{truck}, \emph{hat}, \emph{dog}) matched on frequency in the Pile \citep{gao2020}, counted with infini-gram \citep{liu2024infinigram}, and on count/mass type. Two independent AI coding passes with a fixed prompt rated colour typicality before any model was run (weighted $\kappa$ = 0.96). Each noun was read in three neutral frames, such as \emph{The \textit{noun} was \textit{colour}. The \textit{noun} was}, with the 11 basic colour terms that are one token. Carry is the mean over frames and ordered colour pairs of $\log p(c_1 \mid \text{context } c_1) - \log p(c_1 \mid \text{context } c_2)$, with colour probabilities renormalised over the colour set. We replaced only each noun's input row with a random direction at the norm of a row at initialisation (\emph{atypical\_init}, the counterpart of an untrained row) or with a typical row. We registered that carry would be higher with atypical\_init rows than with original (H45) or typical rows (H46), lower for colour-typical than colour-variable nouns (H47), and that the H45 increase would differ between the groups (H48). Each needed Holm-corrected significance in at least four of the five models.

\textbf{Results.} With their own rows, nouns carried 1.89 to 2.70 nats of colour in the primary models (Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, right). No hypothesis was supported. Atypical\_init rows changed carry by \textminus{}0.38 to +0.34 nats, positive and significant in two models and negative in two others; typical rows likewise (H46 met in two models). Colour-typical and colour-variable nouns carried the same (differences \textminus{}0.16 to 0.11; H47 met in none), and H48 was inconclusive (Table~\ref{tab:N6}). A mixed model over the five models gave no effect of either row (0.00 and \textminus{}0.04 nats) or of group. Changing only the norm of a replaced row changed carry by less than 0.01 nats on average in every primary model. In Pythia-1.4B, carry rose over training, from 0.73 nats at step 1,000 to 2.10 at the end (exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:N8}).

\section{Discussion}
In our small transformers, a familiar noun kept taking its shape from the context only when its own contexts had made that useful. When a noun's sentences came singly, carry fell within about 100 exposures whatever the noun had been seen with. When they came in runs that shared a shape, and the shape changed between runs, the noun still carried 3.85 nats at 1,000 exposures. In Regular models, runs that gave no shape, or always the same stored shape, kept little by 1,000 exposures. So recurrence on its own was not enough. Our reading is that what mattered was whether the noun's earlier sentences were the only reliable source of its current shape. In Reassigned models, where the trained kinds teach that a stored shape can be wrong, fixed nouns in runs also kept 3.57 nats at 1,000 exposures (exploratory). These are the conditions that favour in-context learning for whole classes, bursty items whose meaning changes \citep{chan2022}, and here they decided carry for a single noun within one model. Experiment~1's conclusion, that familiarity alone switched carry off, holds only for familiar nouns whose contexts were never useful.

Stored knowledge also lowered carry, by about 1 nat with frames matched in Regular models, but not in Reassigned models, where a kind's stored shape is unreliable. A noun with a stored shape that its context can contradict may therefore keep using the context.

Unseen nouns are not a neutral baseline. Their carry depended on rows unlike those of trained nouns: rescaling those rows changed it a little, and moving a row to where trained rows lie removed it, which fits the finding that untrained embeddings preserve in-context use \citep{anand2025}. For work with nonce words, this means an unseen token's representation can carry much of an effect, and effects should be checked with representations that look familiar.

The pretrained check did not find any of this. In Pythia, real nouns took a moderate amount of colour from the context, the same for colour-typical and colour-variable nouns, and replacing their input rows had no consistent effect; the norm of the replaced row made almost no difference. Our design cannot say why. The models are far larger, the nouns have rich histories, and colour after a noun is a weaker test than our controlled shape slot. We report the result as a failure to generalise the row effect, not as evidence about pretrained models' use of context in general.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three dimensions and a few frames; the small models are trained with one recipe. In Experiment~2, runs and singles were matched in sentences but not in the number or spacing of occasions, and shape-absent nouns expected a colour at the test position. The run effects for shape-absent and fixed nouns were confined to 100 exposures, and the fixed-noun contrast could not be interpreted (K1). Overall carry differed between the two experiments, so we compare only within each. H30 failed, and the effects of stored knowledge in Experiment~1 are exploratory. Power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57, below plan. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs, three frames and colour only, and its colour-typicality codes came from two AI passes, not human coders. The head search used one signature and mean ablation. Earlier experiments were registered in stages, some after related results were known (Appendix~\ref{app:A}); Experiments 1 and 2 were registered before any of their corpora was built, and every registered test is reported.
