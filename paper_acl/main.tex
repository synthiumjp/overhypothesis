\title{Whether Small Transformers Copy a Feature from Context\\Depends on the Noun's Training History}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A language model can predict a word's properties by copying them from the context or by recalling what it has stored. We ask what in a single word's training history decides which. We trained 150 small transformers on an artificial language about objects' shapes, colours and textures, and controlled how often 40 new nouns occurred, with which shapes, and whether their sentences came one at a time or in short bursts. Copying was measured as how much more probable (in nats) the model made a shape named in the preceding sentence. Never-seen nouns copied, but mainly because their input embeddings were untrained. In corpora where object kinds keep their shapes, nouns met one sentence at a time lost most of their copying with exposure, whether or not they had stored a shape. Nouns met in bursts that shared a shape, with the shape changing between bursts, kept copying: 3.4 nats more than the same sentences spaced out (pre-registered), and 2.9 nats more after 1,000 sentences than bursts with a fixed shape (exploratory). This copying was not tied to the noun. When kinds' shapes changed across the corpus, all bursts kept copying. In Pythia, replacing nouns' embeddings had no consistent effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a language model reads \emph{The car was red. The car was}, it can predict \emph{red} because the context says so, or predict a typical car colour from what it has stored. Which source it relies on matters for how models handle documents that contradict their knowledge \citep{longpre2021,xie2024}, for how they learn new words and tokens, and for how we interpret probes that use invented words. Work on in-context and in-weights learning shows that the balance depends on the training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning from context; common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights \citep{chan2022,chan2025}. Inside pretrained models, mechanisms that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask the same question about a single word: \emph{what in a word's own training history decides whether a model copies its features from the context?}

Three accounts are plausible, and they make different predictions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Memory competition.} A model copies until it has stored something about the word; stored knowledge then competes with the context. Words with a stored feature should stop copying sooner than words without one.
\item \textbf{Representation.} Copying for new words reflects their unusual, untrained embeddings. Once a word's embedding looks like those of familiar words, copying should stop, whatever the word was seen with.
\item \textbf{Useful context.} A model learns, word by word, whether copying has paid off. A word should keep being copied if, in training, its own earlier mentions predicted its features and memory could not.
\end{itemize}
Natural data cannot separate these, because frequent words are both familiar and well known. Invented (nonce) words, the usual probe \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}, are new in both respects at once, and conclusions drawn with them can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}.

We therefore built the separation into training. Small transformers learned an artificial language describing objects by shape, colour and texture, into which we inserted 40 new nouns. For each noun we set how often it occurred, which shapes it occurred with, and whether its sentences came one at a time or in short bursts (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). We measured how strongly each model copied a shape named in one sentence into the next mention of a noun. A first pre-registered experiment tested the memory and representation accounts. A second, also pre-registered, removed two differences between new nouns and the language's own nouns that the first left open, and tested the useful-context account. Last, we asked whether the effect of a word's embedding carries over to pretrained models.

In corpora where object kinds keep their shapes, the results favour the useful-context account, built on the representation account. New nouns copied mainly because of their untrained embeddings. Nouns met one sentence at a time lost most of their copying, whether or not they had a stored shape. Nouns whose earlier mentions had been the sole source of their current shape kept copying. What they kept was a tendency, stored in the noun's embedding, to copy the latest shape, not a link between the noun and its shape. When the corpus as a whole made shapes changeable, bursts of any kind kept copying. Our claims are about small transformers trained on this language; we note parallels with children's word learning in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}; it depends on task diversity \citep{raventos2023}, favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023}. Its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}, and in small transformers stored bigram statistics are learned quickly and in-context ones later, through an induction head \citep{bietti2023}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. We manipulate frequency, stored knowledge and arrangement separately for single words within one model.

\textbf{Copying and recall.} Language models have long gained from copying recent words, through caches and pointers \citep{kuhn1990,grave2017,merity2017}, because words recur in bursts: a word that has occurred once is far more likely to occur again \citep{church1995,church2000}, a regularity that human memory also tracks \citep{anderson1991}. Pretrained models answer from memory more for frequent entities \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023,razeghi2022} and resist contexts that contradict what they know \citep{longpre2021,xie2024,du2024}; copying and recall compete inside the network \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}.

\textbf{New words and new tokens.} Rare and new words have poor representations \citep{schick2020}, under-trained tokens behave abnormally \citep{land2024}, and new embeddings are often initialised at the mean of existing ones \citep{hewitt2021}. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and when language models acquire a word depends mainly on its frequency \citep{chang2022}. Children's word learning offers related evidence on shape \citep{landau1988,smith2002}, on expectations about how features vary \citep{kemp2007,dewar2010} and on the timing of exposures \citep{childers2002,schwab2016}, to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by an invented noun (the \emph{trained kinds}), with 14 exemplars per kind. An exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten invented words, and is described in one of five sentence frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture) or \emph{Look at the wug. It is zibby and lavo} (Appendix~\ref{app:I}). The training text runs through all exemplars many times, each pass arranged afresh. In \textbf{stable-shape} corpora each kind keeps one shape, so a kind's shape can be stored. In \textbf{shifting-shape} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass and keeps it within the pass, so its current shape can come only from the context. In both, sentences about a kind come in runs of four; a control corpus uses random order. Every word is one token.

\textbf{Models.} Decoder-only transformers with pre-layer normalisation and learned position embeddings, with 3.4M, 11.0M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers), read the text once in non-overlapping windows of 128 tokens. Input and output embeddings are untied, so a noun's input embedding (its \emph{row}) carries everything the model has learned about the noun as an input. We trained ten seeds of each size (Appendix~\ref{app:C}).

\textbf{Controlling a noun's history.} We added 40 new nouns to each corpus. Four were never used in training (\emph{unseen}); the others are \emph{familiar} in the sense that they occur in training. Each familiar noun had one history (Table~\ref{tab:design}) at a set number of exposures, counted in sentences, with three nouns per condition, rotated over nouns across seeds. A noun's sentences were spread evenly from 5\% to 95\% of training, and each lay wholly inside one window. Presented \emph{spaced}, a noun has one sentence per window. Presented in \emph{bursts}, it has four consecutive sentences in one window, so the same number of sentences occupies a quarter as many windows.

\textbf{Two experiments.} Experiment~1 (90 models) crossed fixed-, changing- and no-shape nouns, all spaced, with 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, in stable-shape, shifting-shape and random-order corpora. Fixed- and changing-shape nouns appeared in all five frames, and no-shape nouns in a frame that names no shape (\emph{The \textit{noun} is \textit{colour} and \textit{texture}}). In that experiment familiar nouns never recurred within a window, unlike the trained kinds, and no-shape nouns never appeared in the frame used for testing. Experiment~2 (60 models) removed both differences. It crossed the three histories with spaced and burst presentation at 100 and 1,000 exposures, in stable-shape and shifting-shape corpora. Fixed- and changing-shape nouns appeared only in the test frame, and no-shape nouns in that frame without the shape (\emph{A \textit{noun} is a \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}). This frame has a side effect: no-shape nouns learn to expect a colour word, not a shape, after \emph{is a}, by 1 to 5 nats at the test position, whereas every other familiar noun expects a shape (Appendix~\ref{app:N}). Comparisons involving no-shape nouns in Experiment~2 are therefore confounded with expected word class. The experiments also differ in how much of the text the new nouns fill (about 60\% and 8\% of windows in the 3.4M and 25.6M models), so we compare conditions only within an experiment.

\textbf{Measures.} A test item gives one sentence about a noun naming a shape $s$, then the start of the next mention, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the model's log probability $L$ of each of the ten shape words at the next position (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). The \textbf{copy score} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes, leaving out the noun's own shape (a fixed placebo shape for nouns that have none), which $s$ never is. It is in nats: 0 means the context's shape is no more likely than the others, and 3.4 means it is about 30 times as likely as the (geometric) average other shape. Each noun gets 36 items. In a \textbf{control}, the query names another new noun with the same history; if copying is tied to the noun, the control should score lower. \textbf{Knowledge} is $L$ of the noun's own shape minus the mean $L$ of the other nine after an unrelated sentence, also in nats. We also score unseen nouns with a \textbf{typical embedding}: the mean of the trained kinds' rows plus a random offset of typical length.

\textbf{Predictions.} Table~\ref{tab:pred} gives what each account predicts for the comparisons that separate them. The memory account alone predicts that a stored shape lowers copying. The useful-context account alone predicts that changing-shape bursts keep copying more than both changing-shape spaced nouns and fixed-shape bursts.

\textbf{Analysis and registration.} The seed is the unit of replication: we average each condition over the three model sizes within a seed and test across ten seeds, with Holm correction within each experiment, and count a prediction as supported only if its direction also holds in two of the three sizes. Both experiments were registered before any model was trained (Experiment~1 before its corpora were built). Each result below is labelled pre-registered or exploratory. Appendix~\ref{app:map} maps every claim to its registered hypothesis and lists all registered tests and their outcomes.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{New nouns copy because their embeddings are untrained}\label{sec:rows}
In stable-shape models of Experiment~1, unseen nouns had a copy score of 4.53 nats [95\% CI 3.54,~5.52]. Given a typical embedding, the same nouns scored $-$0.33 (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left). We had found the same in earlier experiments with this language (Appendix~\ref{app:L}). To see which property of an untrained row matters, we changed only the rows of unseen nouns in the Experiment~1 models. Scaling a row to the trained kinds' average size, keeping its direction, lowered the score only from 4.53 to 4.00 (pre-registered; almost entirely in the smallest models). A random direction of that size kept copying (4.09), whereas the trained kinds' mean row removed it ($-$0.44; both exploratory). What suppresses copying is therefore what a row shares with trained nouns' rows, not its size. In shifting-shape models, scaling raised the score instead (exploratory). In these small models, then, a probe with an unseen word measures the word's untrained row as much as anything learned about context.

\subsection{Nouns met one at a time lose copying, whatever they store}
Experiment~1 tested the memory and representation accounts. Changing-shape nouns, which never have a shape to store, are the cleanest test of the memory account. In stable-shape models they lost copying as quickly as fixed-shape nouns, which did store their shape (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left): the two stayed within 0.4 nats of each other at every exposure (exploratory). Fixed-shape nouns learned their shape well, preferring it by 8.3 to 9.5 nats after an unrelated sentence at 1,000 exposures (pre-registered manipulation check), yet their copy score did not fall faster than that of no-shape nouns (pre-registered prediction, not supported). For all three histories, copying fell from 3.6 to 4.5 nats at 10 exposures to under 1 nat at 100 and to near zero from 1,000 on. No-shape nouns at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures ended 0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52] above unseen nouns with a typical embedding, a reliable difference that was nonetheless within the registered 1-nat equivalence bound (pre-registered). In random-order corpora no noun copied (exploratory), so copying is learned from the runs of sentences about a kind.

These results reject the memory account and fit the representation account: once a noun was familiar, it lost copying whatever it had stored. In shifting-shape corpora, familiar nouns kept more copying (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, middle; Section~\ref{sec:shifting}). Experiment~2 asked whether recurrence, or a context that is useful, keeps copying alive in stable-shape corpora.

\subsection{Bursts with changing shapes keep copying}
The useful-context account predicts that changing-shape bursts, where a noun's previous sentence gives its current shape and memory cannot, keep copying (Table~\ref{tab:pred}); the other two accounts predict no difference from spaced presentation. Changing-shape bursts scored 3.39 nats [2.69,~4.09] more than changing-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered; Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, left; Table~\ref{tab:key}), as much at 1,000 exposures (3.35) as at 100 (3.43). After 1,000 exposures they still scored 3.85 nats, against 0.5 to 1.0 for every other familiar history and 0.61 for unseen nouns with a typical embedding. They had fallen from 5.02 at 100 exposures, so with two exposure levels we cannot tell whether copying levels off or falls slowly.

Because bursts occupy fewer windows, a burst noun's row is updated less often, which might leave it closer to an untrained row. Fixed-shape bursts control for this: the same frame, presentation and number of windows, but a shape that never changes, so memory can supply it. Changing-shape bursts scored 1.73 nats [1.25,~2.20] more than fixed-shape bursts, and 2.86 [1.99,~3.74] more at 1,000 exposures (exploratory). This is the comparison that separates useful context from both other accounts, but it was not the registered test, and it was largest in the smallest models (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Section~\ref{sec:rowsexp2} examines the rows directly.

Recurrence without a useful context helped less and only early. No-shape bursts scored 0.80 nats [0.47,~1.13] more than no-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered); the registered reading of this outcome was that recurrence preserves copying. Split by exposure (exploratory), the gain was 1.55 nats at 100 exposures and 0.05 [$-$0.30,~0.40] at 1,000. Fixed-shape bursts scored 1.69 nats [1.29,~2.08] more than fixed-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered), but this difference cannot be credited to recurrence: at 100 exposures, bursts also left the stored shape weaker (knowledge 3.0 against 5.4 nats), and the registered check that knowledge be equal failed. At 1,000 exposures, where knowledge was equal, bursts added 0.47 nats [0.27,~0.66] (exploratory), about a sixth of the advantage of changing-shape bursts.

\subsection{A stored shape does not itself reduce copying}
Fixed- and changing-shape nouns presented spaced share frame, presentation and expected word class, and differ only in whether one shape can be stored. Their copy scores were the same: $-$0.02 nats [$-$0.16,~0.11], equivalent to zero within $\pm$0.3 nats (exploratory, TOST $p<.001$), as in Experiment~1. The registered comparisons with no-shape nouns point the other way but are confounded. Fixed-shape spaced nouns scored 1.02 nats [0.76,~1.27] below no-shape spaced nouns, and no-shape spaced nouns scored 1.42 [1.12,~1.73] above unseen nouns with a typical embedding, not equivalent to them (both pre-registered). Since no-shape nouns expect a colour where the others expect a shape, these differences reflect expected word class, not a stored shape. A stored shape mattered only through the context: fixed-shape bursts, whose earlier sentences predicted a shape that memory also supplied, lost copying, and changing-shape bursts did not.

\subsection{History sets whether the model copies, not what it copies}
If changing-shape bursts taught the model to bind a noun to its current shape, the control, in which the query names another noun, should score lower. It did not. In stable-shape models, the copy score with a context about the queried noun exceeded the control by at most 0.07 nats in any Experiment~2 condition, and 0.14 in Experiment~1 (exploratory). The model copied the most recent shape after the queried noun, whichever noun the preceding sentence described. In training, every sentence of a burst followed one about the same noun, so nothing required the model to check which noun a shape belonged to.

We registered a search for heads that carry the shape: ablate the heads with the highest product of copying and prefix-matching scores, the signature of induction heads \citep{olsson2022}. No head matched prefixes clearly better than uniform attention (highest score 0.043, against 0.027), so the candidates were first-layer copying heads and the search could not test what it was registered to test. Ablating them lowered unseen nouns' copy score from 4.53 to 2.04 nats on average, against 2.68 for random heads, a difference that was not reliable; the registered prediction that they remove more than half of copying was not supported (Appendix~\ref{app:M}). The mechanism remains open.

\subsection{The copying lives in the noun's row}\label{sec:rowsexp2}
A noun enters the model only through its row, so whatever its history taught must be stored there. The question raised by bursts is whether changing-shape bursts simply leave the row less trained. We examined every familiar noun's row in the Experiment~2 models (exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:N13}). Rows of nouns met in bursts stayed closer to their initial values than rows of spaced nouns, and at 100 exposures closeness to the initial row predicted copying well: once row geometry was taken into account, changing-shape bursts copied no more than other histories. By 1,000 exposures this no longer held. Changing-shape burst rows were only slightly closer to their initial values than fixed-shape burst rows (cosine 0.85 against 0.78), and from row geometry alone they would be expected to score 0.74 nats; they scored 3.85. Swapping rows between the two histories moved the copying with the row: changing-shape burst nouns given fixed-shape burst rows scored 0.97, and fixed-shape burst nouns given changing-shape burst rows 3.86. First-layer attention from the query to the context's shape tracked copying across nouns (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.81) and was higher for changing-shape bursts. Bursts thus first preserve copying by leaving rows near their initial state, and changing-shape bursts then give the row a copying tendency of its own.

This learned copying is narrow. With one unrelated sentence between the context and the query, no noun copied (at most 0.37 nats). With the context in another frame (\emph{Look at the \textit{noun}. It is \textit{shape} and \textit{colour}.}), changing-shape bursts kept about 40\% of their copying (1.53 nats at 1,000 exposures, 1.21 more than fixed-shape bursts). For colour, which varies freely within every kind, no noun copied.

\subsection{When the whole corpus rewards copying}\label{sec:shifting}
These conclusions hold for stable-shape corpora. In shifting-shape corpora, where the trained kinds' own shapes can be recovered only from context, copying behaved differently (Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, right; all exploratory). Part of it was tied to the noun: a context about the queried noun raised the copy score over the control by 0.48 nats for unseen nouns and 1.45 for changing-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures. Every burst history kept copying, not only changing-shape bursts: at 1,000 exposures, fixed-shape bursts scored 3.57 nats and no-shape bursts 2.09, although bursts again weakened stored shapes. And changing-shape bursts copied more than unseen nouns (6.82 against 5.02). What a noun's history teaches therefore depends on what the rest of the corpus teaches about stored features: when the corpus rewards copying, recurrence alone sustains it.

\subsection{Model size}\label{sec:scale}
Most effects were largest in the smallest models (Table~\ref{tab:scale}). From 3.4M to 25.6M parameters, the advantage of changing-shape bursts over spaced presentation fell from 5.05 to 2.29 nats and over fixed-shape bursts from 2.94 to 1.00 nats, while unseen nouns with a typical embedding copied more (from $-$0.30 to 1.51). All effects kept their direction at every size, but over this range they shrink as models grow.

\subsection{Pretrained models}\label{sec:pythia}
Finally, we asked whether the effect of an untrained embedding carries over to pretrained models, whose nouns have long histories. We registered three predictions for Pythia models from 1B to 12B parameters \citep{biderman2023}: that replacing a noun's embedding with a random one of untrained scale would raise copying, more than replacing it with a typical one, and, by the memory account, that nouns with a typical colour would copy less than nouns without one. Each needed support in four of the five models. We read 22 pairs of one-token nouns, one colour-typical (\emph{sky}, \emph{leaf}, \emph{bone}) and one colour-variable (\emph{truck}, \emph{hat}, \emph{dog}), matched on frequency in the Pile \citep{gao2020,liu2024infinigram} and on count or mass use, in frames such as \emph{The \textit{noun} was \textit{colour}. The \textit{noun} was}. Because real nouns have colour priors, the copy score here compares contexts: the log probability of a colour after a context naming it, minus that after a context naming another colour, averaged over the 11 basic colour words. The models had stored typical colours: with no context, colour-typical nouns preferred their colour by 1.3 to 1.7 nats, and human colour norms \citep{paik2021} agreed with our coding for 15 of the 16 colour-typical nouns they cover (both exploratory).

Real nouns copied 1.9 to 2.7 nats of colour (Figure~\ref{fig:pythia}). None of the predictions held (pre-registered). The random replacement raised copying reliably in two models and lowered it in two others, by up to 0.4 nats; the typical replacement changed it by up to 0.6 nats in either direction; and colour-typical and colour-variable nouns did not differ reliably. Most changes were also not small enough to count as zero, so the result is inconsistent rather than null. Changing only the size of the random replacement changed copying by less than 0.01 nats (exploratory). In the smaller, descriptive models the pattern differed again: at 160M parameters the random replacement doubled copying. The untrained-embedding effect of the small models did not transfer to these models and this probe.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What decides copying.} In stable-shape corpora our results fit a combined account. Whether the model copies the latest shape after a noun depends on the noun's row. The tendency starts high for rows far from the trained nouns' rows, which is why unseen nouns copy and lose it with a typical row. Training removes it, whatever is stored, unless the noun's own earlier sentences were the only source of its current shape: changing-shape bursts kept copying where fixed-shape bursts with the same recurrence did not. The memory account failed: a stored shape neither sped up the loss of copying nor lowered it once expected word class was matched. Row analyses support this: early on, bursts keep copying by keeping rows near their initial state; later, changing-shape bursts give the row a copying tendency that geometry alone does not explain and that moves with the row. Whether it lasts beyond 1,000 exposures we have not shown.

\textbf{What is expected and what is not.} That a model copies where copying lowers its loss is expected. Two things are less so. Recurrence alone did not keep copying in stable-shape corpora: no-shape bursts lost their advantage over spaced presentation by 1,000 exposures. And a stored shape did not compete with the context unless the context also repeated it. That copying was not tied to the noun fits training in which it never needed to be. Together, this is the population-level finding of \citet{chan2022}, that bursty items whose meaning changes favour learning in context, acting on single words within one model, but only when the rest of the corpus does not already reward copying.

\textbf{Implications.} These implications are for small models trained this way; Pythia did not show the embedding effect. Probes with invented words can measure an unseen token's untrained row more than anything learned about context, so effects should be checked with rows that look familiar. If the same holds in larger models, initialising new tokens at the mean of existing embeddings \citep{hewitt2021} would weaken the copying that lets a model learn them from context, and entities whose attributes are stable within documents but vary across them would be the ones a model most readily takes from context. Testing this needs measures of each word's burstiness in pretraining.

\textbf{Analogies with children.} Two findings echo research on how children learn words, which we note as analogies only. Changing-shape bursts have the structure of the boxes in \citet{dewar2010}: objects uniform within a box, varying between boxes. After one draw from a new box, 9-month-olds expected the next to match, an expectation about how a feature varies across groups, which \citet{kemp2007} call an overhypothesis. And at 100 exposures, spaced nouns stored their shape more strongly than nouns met in bursts, while bursts kept the context in use. Children likewise retain words and categories better from spaced than from massed exposures \citep{childers2002,vlach2008}, although they learn new labels better when the labels are repeated in successive sentences \citep{schwab2016}.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three features and five sentence frames; the models are small and trained with one recipe, and most effects shrank with model size. The comparison that best separates useful context from the other accounts was exploratory. Experiment~2 had two exposure levels, so we cannot tell whether copying in changing-shape bursts levels off. The learned copying reached back only one sentence, transferred only partly to another frame, and did not apply to colour. No-shape comparisons in Experiment~2 are confounded with expected word class, and bursts weakened stored shapes at 100 exposures. The other new nouns in a corpus may affect a noun's copying, and we did not train models with a single history. The unseen-noun result depends on untied embeddings, which leave unseen rows untrained. The head search could not test its hypothesis, and the mechanism is unknown. Planned power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57, below the target of 0.8. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs, three frames and colour only. Earlier experiments in this language were registered in stages, some after related results were known (Appendix~\ref{app:A}).
