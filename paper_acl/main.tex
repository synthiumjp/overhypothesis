\title{Whether Small Transformers Copy a Feature from Context\\Depends on the Noun's Training History}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A language model can predict a word's properties by copying them from context or by recalling what it stored. Across populations of items, bursty items whose meaning changes favour copying; we test this word by word within one model. We trained 230 small transformers on an artificial language about objects, controlling how often new nouns occurred, with which shapes, and whether their sentences came one at a time or in bursts. Nouns kept copying a shape from the preceding sentence only where, in training, their own earlier sentences had predicted a shape that memory could not. In a pre-registered replication, nouns met in bursts whose shape changed between bursts copied 3.7 nats more after 1,000 sentences than nouns in fixed-shape bursts (4.2 nats at 85.6M parameters). Memory acted mainly during training: at test, a stored shape lowered copying by only 0.25 nats, though the model still preferred the stored shape. What bursts kept was a tendency to copy the latest shape, not a link between noun and shape, and it spread to other nouns. Never-seen nouns copied mainly because their embeddings were untrained. Replacing embeddings in Pythia had no consistent effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a language model reads \emph{The car was red. The car was}, it can predict \emph{red} because the context says so, or predict a typical car colour from what it has stored. Which source it relies on matters for how models handle documents that contradict their knowledge \citep{longpre2021,xie2024}, for how they learn new words and tokens, and for how we interpret probes that use invented words. Work on in-context and in-weights learning shows that the balance depends on the training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning from context; common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights (\citealp{chan2022}; \citealp{chan2025}). Inside pretrained models, mechanisms that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask the same question about a single word: \emph{what in a word's own training history decides whether a model copies its features from the context?}

Three accounts are plausible, and they make different predictions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Memory competition.} When the model predicts, a stored feature competes with the context. Words with a stored feature should copy less than words without one.
\item \textbf{Representation.} New words copy because their embeddings are untrained. Once a word's embedding looks like those of familiar words, copying should stop, whatever the word was seen with.
\item \textbf{Useful context.} During training, the model learns for each word whether copying has paid off. A word should keep being copied if its own earlier mentions predicted its features and memory could not.
\end{itemize}
Natural data cannot separate these, because frequent words are both familiar and well known. Invented (nonce) words, the usual probe \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}, are new in both respects at once, and conclusions drawn with them can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}.

We therefore built the separation into training. Small transformers learned an artificial language describing objects by shape, colour and texture, into which we inserted 40 new nouns. For each noun we set how often it occurred, which shapes it occurred with, and whether its sentences came one at a time or in short bursts (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). We measured how strongly each model copied a shape named in one sentence into the next mention of a noun. Three pre-registered experiments, the third a replication on new models, separate the accounts.

The results join two of them. Unseen nouns copy because their embeddings are untrained. Familiar nouns lose copying unless their own earlier sentences were the only source of their current shape, as in bursts whose shape changes between bursts. Memory mattered, but mainly during training: where a shape could be stored, the context stopped being needed, and copying was lost. At test, a stored shape barely lowered copying, although the model still preferred the stored shape. What useful bursts kept was a tendency, carried by the noun's embedding, to copy the latest shape, not a link between noun and shape, and it spread to other nouns. Our claims are about small transformers trained on this language; we note parallels with children's word learning in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}; what is learned in context also generalises differently \citep{chan2022generalize}. It depends on task diversity \citep{raventos2023}, favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023}. Its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}, and in small transformers stored bigram statistics are learned quickly and in-context ones later \citep{bietti2023}. Synthetic languages and Markov chains have been used to trace how in-context learning forms \citep{akyurek2024,edelman2024}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. We manipulate frequency, stored knowledge and arrangement separately for single words within one model.

\textbf{Copying and recall.} Language models have long gained from copying recent words, through caches and pointers \citep{kuhn1990,grave2017,merity2017}, because words recur in bursts \citep{church1995,church2000,madsen2005}, a regularity that human memory also tracks \citep{anderson1991}. Pretrained models answer from memory more for frequent entities \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023,razeghi2022} and resist contexts that contradict what they know \citep{longpre2021,xie2024,du2024}; copying and recall compete inside the network \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}.

\textbf{New words and new tokens.} Rare and new words have poor representations \citep{schick2020}, and good embeddings for them can be built from a few contexts \citep{herbelot2017,khodak2018}; under-trained tokens behave abnormally \citep{land2024}, new embeddings are often initialised at the mean of existing ones \citep{hewitt2021}, and tying input and output embeddings \citep{press2017,inan2017} trains every token's embedding as an output. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and when language models acquire a word depends mainly on its frequency \citep{chang2022}. Children's word learning offers related evidence on shape \citep{landau1988,smith2002}, on expectations about how features vary \citep{kemp2007,dewar2010} and on the timing of exposures \citep{childers2002,schwab2016}, to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by an invented noun (the \emph{trained kinds}), with 14 exemplars per kind. An exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten invented words, and is described in one of five sentence frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture) or \emph{Look at the wug. It is zibby and lavo} (Appendix~\ref{app:lang}). The corpus runs through all exemplars many times, each pass arranged afresh, and a model reads it once. Sentences about a kind come in runs of four. In \textbf{stable-kind} corpora each kind keeps one shape, so its shape can be stored. In \textbf{shifting-kind} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass, so its current shape can come only from the context. Every word is one token.

\textbf{Models.} Decoder-only transformers with pre-layer normalisation and learned position embeddings, with 3.4M, 10.9M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers), and in Experiment~3 also 85.6M (12 layers), read the text once in windows of 128 tokens. Input and output embeddings are untied, so a noun's input embedding carries everything the model has learned about the noun as an input; we chose this after finding that earlier models in this language had been trained untied by a hardware fault (Appendix~\ref{app:train}). We also check tied embeddings.

\textbf{Controlling a noun's history.} We added 40 new nouns to each corpus. Four never occurred in training (\emph{unseen}); the other 36 did (\emph{familiar}). Each familiar noun had one history (Table~\ref{tab:design}) and a set number of sentences (\emph{exposures}), spread evenly from 5\% to 95\% of training, with three nouns per condition. Presented \emph{spaced}, a noun has one sentence per window. Presented in \emph{bursts}, it has four consecutive sentences in one window, like the trained kinds' runs.

\textbf{Three experiments} (Table~\ref{tab:exps}). Experiment~1 tested the memory and representation accounts with spaced nouns. In it, familiar nouns never recurred within a window, unlike the trained kinds, and no-shape nouns never appeared in the test frame. Experiment~2 removed both differences and added bursts, to test the useful-context account. It used 100 and 1,000 exposures, where Experiment~1's copying had nearly vanished. One side effect: no-shape nouns, which now appeared in the test frame without a shape, learned to expect a colour after \emph{is a} (by 1 to 5 nats), so comparisons with them are confounded with word class. Experiment~3 was registered after Experiment~2's results to test them on new models: ten new seeds at the three sizes, ten models of 85.6M parameters, and five seeds in single-history corpora. New nouns fill about 60\% of training windows in Experiment~1 and 8\% in Experiments~2 and~3, so we compare conditions only within an experiment.

\textbf{Measures.} A test item gives one sentence about a noun naming a shape $s$, then the start of the next mention, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the model's log probability $L$ of each of the ten shapes at the next position (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). The \textbf{copy score} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes, leaving out the noun's own shape $s^*$, which the context never names (nouns without a shape get a fixed stand-in). It is in nats: 3.4 means the context's shape is about 30 times as likely as the average other shape. Each noun gets 36 items. Because the copy score leaves out $s^*$, we also report which shape the model prefers, $L(s^*) - L(s)$, and how strongly it has stored $s^*$: $L(s^*)$ minus the mean of the other shapes after an unrelated sentence (\textbf{stored preference}). In a \textbf{control}, the query names another new noun with the same history; if copying is tied to the noun, the control should score lower. We also score unseen nouns with a \textbf{typical embedding}: the mean of the trained kinds' embeddings plus a random offset of typical length.

\textbf{Predictions.} Table~\ref{tab:pred} gives what each account predicts. Only memory competition predicts that a stored shape lowers copying among spaced nouns. Only useful context predicts that changing-shape bursts copy more than changing-shape spaced nouns, unless bursts help simply by updating the embedding less often. Fixed-shape bursts test that: they get as many updates, but their shape can be stored, so the context is not needed. Memory competition also predicts that they copy less, but only by as much as a stored shape lowers copying among spaced nouns.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
The confirmatory core is Experiment~3's registered replication (Sections~\ref{sec:bursts}--\ref{sec:single}): changing-shape bursts copy more than spaced and fixed-shape bursts, a stored shape barely lowers copying but wins in prediction, the copying is not tied to the noun, the queried noun's history matters most, the effect exceeds what embedding geometry predicts, and it appears without nouns of other histories. The mechanism analyses, shifting-kind corpora, tied embeddings and natural text are exploratory. Results not marked pre-registered are exploratory.

\subsection{New nouns copy because their embeddings are untrained}\label{sec:rows}
In stable-kind models of Experiment~1, unseen nouns had a copy score of 4.53 nats [95\% CI 3.54,~5.52]. Given a typical embedding, the same nouns scored $-$0.33 (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left); in Experiment~3 the drop was from 6.88 to 0.79. To see which property of an untrained embedding matters, we changed only the embeddings of unseen nouns in the Experiment~1 models. Scaling one to the trained kinds' average size, keeping its direction, lowered the score from 4.53 to 4.00 (pre-registered and supported, almost entirely in the smallest models). A random direction of that size kept copying (4.09), whereas the trained kinds' mean embedding removed it ($-$0.44). Size thus matters a little; what an embedding shares with trained nouns' embeddings matters most. With tied embeddings, which train unseen nouns' embeddings as outputs (ten 3.4M models), unseen nouns still copied (3.96 nats), a typical embedding still removed it (0.02), and changing-shape bursts still copied more than fixed-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures (1.90 [1.20,~2.60]), though all effects were smaller (Appendix~\ref{app:O}). In these small models, then, a probe with an unseen word measures the word's embedding as much as anything learned about context.

\subsection{Nouns met one at a time lose copying}
Changing-shape nouns never have a shape to store, so they are the cleanest test of memory competition. In stable-kind models of Experiment~1 they lost copying almost as quickly as fixed-shape nouns, which stored their shape well (stored preference 9.5 nats at 1,000 exposures; Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left). Fixed-shape nouns did not lose copying faster than no-shape nouns (pre-registered prediction, not supported). For all three histories, copying fell from 3.6--4.5 nats at 10 exposures to under 1 nat at 100 and to near zero from 1,000 on. No-shape nouns ended within the registered bound of unseen nouns with a typical embedding (0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52]; pre-registered). In random-order corpora no noun copied, so copying is learned from the runs of sentences about a kind. Once a noun was familiar, it lost copying largely whatever it had stored, as the representation account predicts.

\subsection{Bursts with changing shapes keep copying}\label{sec:bursts}
Changing-shape bursts, where a noun's previous sentence gives its current shape and memory cannot, scored 4.14 nats [3.60,~4.69] more than changing-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered; Figure~\ref{fig:a11}a; Table~\ref{tab:key}), as much at 1,000 exposures as at 100. After 1,000 exposures they still scored 4.71 nats, against at most 1.1 for every other familiar history and 0.79 for unseen nouns with a typical embedding. Over training this copying did not fade: in Experiment~2, for nouns with 1,000 exposures, it rose from 0.8--2.6 nats at the first saved weights to 3.85 at the end, while every other history ended near or below 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:a10formation}).

Fixed-shape bursts have the same frame, presentation and number of updates, but a shape that memory can supply. At 1,000 exposures changing-shape bursts copied 3.68 nats [2.99,~4.37] more than fixed-shape bursts, at all three sizes (pre-registered). Memory competition cannot explain this: among spaced nouns at 1,000 exposures, a stored shape lowered copying by only 0.10, so the burst difference exceeded the spaced difference by 3.58 nats [2.94,~4.22]. What decided copying was whether the context had been needed in training.

Recurrence without a useful context helped less and only early. No-shape bursts scored 0.80 nats [0.47,~1.13] more than no-shape spaced nouns in Experiment~2 (pre-registered), but only at 100 exposures; at 1,000 the gain was 0.05 [$-$0.30,~0.40] (Experiment~3: 0.20). Fixed-shape bursts also copied more than fixed-shape spaced nouns, but at 100 exposures bursts left the stored shape weaker (stored preference 3.0 against 5.1 to 5.4 nats), so this registered comparison is not informative; at 1,000, with equal stored preference, bursts added about 0.5 nats.

\subsection{A stored shape barely lowers copying but wins in prediction}
Fixed- and changing-shape spaced nouns share frame, presentation and expected word class, and differ only in whether a shape can be stored. Fixed-shape spaced nouns copied 0.25 nats [0.15,~0.35] less (pre-registered), mostly at 100 exposures (0.40) rather than 1,000 (0.10). The difference was small but real; Experiment~1 found 0.30 at 1,000 exposures and Experiment~2 none ($-$0.02). The copy score leaves out the stored shape, so it shows only whether the stored shape pushes down the copied one. Which shape the model finally predicts is another matter: fixed-shape spaced nouns preferred their own shape to the context's by 5.84 nats [5.57,~6.11] (pre-registered), 4.1 at 100 exposures and 7.6 at 1,000. These nouns copied little in any case (under 1.6 nats), so the test is limited to weak copying. Fixed-shape spaced nouns also scored about 1 nat below no-shape spaced nouns ($-$1.02, pre-registered, and $-$1.00), but no-shape nouns expect a colour where the others expect a shape, so this difference reflects word class.

\subsection{History sets whether the model copies, not what it copies}
If changing-shape bursts taught the model to bind a noun to its current shape, the control, in which the query names another noun, should score lower. It did not. For changing-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures the context about the queried noun added 0.08 nats [0.02,~0.14] over the control, within $\pm$0.3 (pre-registered), and no stable-kind condition in any experiment exceeded 0.14. The model copied the most recent shape after the queried noun, whichever noun the preceding sentence described. This is unsurprising, since in training every sentence of a burst followed one about the same noun. To see whose history matters, we paired a context about a noun of one history with a query naming a noun of another. At 1,000 exposures, a queried noun with changing-shape bursts copied 1.72 nats [1.36,~2.08] more than one with fixed-shape bursts. The same contrast for the context noun added only 0.47 [0.36,~0.58]. The difference, 1.25 [0.94,~1.56], was pre-registered.

\subsection{Copying spreads between nouns}\label{sec:single}
Because copying is not tied to the noun, what changing-shape bursts teach may reach other nouns. In Experiment~3's single-history corpora no noun of another history was present (Figure~\ref{fig:a11}b). There, changing-shape bursts copied 5.51 nats [4.24,~6.78] more than fixed-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures, at all three sizes (pre-registered), so the main difference does not need nouns of the other history. It was larger than in mixed corpora with the same seeds (4.27), mainly because fixed-shape bursts lost all their copying when no noun of another history shared the corpus: 0.03 nats, against 1.03 in mixed corpora, a difference of 1.00 [0.80,~1.20] (pre-registered). For changing-shape bursts this comparison was inconclusive. Unseen nouns also copied less in fixed-only corpora (4.87, against 6.16 in changing-only and 7.35 in mixed corpora with the same seeds). How much the corpus as a whole rewards copying thus shapes every new noun's copying.

\subsection{How the embedding sets copying}\label{sec:rowsexp2}
A noun enters the model only through its embedding, so whatever its history taught must be carried there. Could changing-shape bursts simply leave the embedding less trained? For every familiar noun we measured the embedding's cosine with its initial value, its distance from the trained kinds' mean and its norm, and regressed copy scores on them within each model. At 1,000 exposures changing-shape bursts copied 3.67 nats [3.03,~4.32] more than these measures predict (pre-registered), although their embeddings were only slightly closer to their initial values than those of fixed-shape bursts (cosine 0.85 against 0.78). At 100 exposures geometry still accounted for their copying (0.45 [$-$0.25,~1.15]). Bursts thus first preserve copying by leaving embeddings near their initial state, and changing-shape bursts then give the embedding a copying tendency of its own.

Three exploratory analyses of the Experiment~3 models locate it. First, it lies along a direction shared by nouns: adding to a noun's embedding the difference between the mean embeddings of changing- and fixed-shape bursts, taken from other nouns, raised copying at 1,000 exposures by 1.51 nats [1.20,~1.82] for fixed-shape spaced nouns and 1.99 for unseen nouns with a typical embedding, while a random vector of the same length changed it by at most 0.02 (Table~\ref{tab:O4}). The effect was largest at 3.4M (2.41 for fixed-shape spaced nouns) and smaller at 25.6M (1.00). Second, the noun's history is read early. Patching the queried noun's hidden state between a changing- and a fixed-shape burst noun \citep{zhang2024patching} showed that after the second block the noun's own position no longer mattered and the effect had moved to the position where the shape is predicted. Third, first-layer attention from that position to the context's shape word tracked copying across conditions within each model (mean Spearman $\rho$ = 0.80 [0.76,~0.85]). Changing-shape bursts thus move the embedding along a direction that goes with more first-layer attention to the latest shape.

This learned copying is narrow. With one unrelated sentence between context and query it almost vanished (at most 0.44 nats). With the context in another frame (\emph{Look at the \textit{noun}. It is \textit{shape} and \textit{colour}.}) changing-shape bursts kept 35 to 40\% of it at 1,000 exposures. For colour, which varies freely within every kind, no noun copied.

\subsection{When the whole corpus rewards copying}\label{sec:shifting}
In Experiment~2's shifting-kind corpora, where the trained kinds' shapes can come only from context, every burst history kept copying (Figure~\ref{fig:a11}c): at 1,000 exposures fixed-shape bursts scored 3.57 nats, no-shape bursts 2.09 and changing-shape bursts 6.82, more than unseen nouns (5.02). When the corpus rewards copying, recurrence alone sustains it.

\subsection{Model size}\label{sec:scale}
In Experiment~3, the advantage of changing- over fixed-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures was 6.36, 2.44 and 2.24 nats at 3.4M, 10.9M and 25.6M parameters, while unseen nouns with a typical embedding copied more as models grew ($-$0.19, 1.20 and 1.37). The decline did not continue. In ten models of 85.6M parameters the advantage was 4.18 nats [2.95,~5.41] (pre-registered); changing-shape bursts still copied 4.85 nats after 1,000 exposures, against at most 1.2 for every other familiar history. These models also had a longer warm-up and other hardware, so size is not their only difference.

\subsection{Pretrained models}\label{sec:pythia}
We registered three predictions for Pythia models from 1B to 12B parameters \citep{biderman2023}, using 22 one-token nouns with a typical colour (\emph{sky}) and 22 without (\emph{truck}), matched on frequency in the Pile \citep{gao2020,liu2024infinigram}: replacing a noun's embedding with a random one of untrained scale would raise colour copying, more than a typical embedding would, and colour-typical nouns would copy less. The models had stored typical colours, and real nouns copied 1.9 to 2.7 nats of colour. None of the predictions held (pre-registered): replacements changed copying by up to 0.6 nats in either direction, inconsistently across models, and most changes were not small enough to count as none (Appendix~\ref{app:N}, Figure~\ref{fig:pythia}).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What decides copying.} In stable-kind corpora, whether the model copies the latest shape after a noun depends on the noun's embedding. The tendency starts high for embeddings far from the trained nouns', which is why unseen nouns copy. Training removes it unless the noun's own earlier sentences were the only source of its current shape. This held in new seeds, at 85.6M parameters and in corpora without nouns of the other history. Memory mattered, but in training rather than at test: where a shape could be stored, the context was not needed, and copying was lost. At test a stored shape lowered copying by 0 to 0.4 nats across three experiments, about a tenth of the effect of a useful context, and acted mainly by winning in prediction. The useful-context and memory accounts are thus two sides of one process: the model keeps copying for a word only where memory could not do the job.

\textbf{What this adds.} \citet{chan2022} showed that bursty items whose meaning changes favour in-context learning across a population. We find the same rule acting on single words within one model, through each word's embedding. Two results go beyond it. Recurrence alone did not keep copying: no-shape bursts lost their advantage by 1,000 exposures. And copying learned for some words spread to others, so a word's tendency to copy depends on the company it keeps in training. When the corpus as a whole rewards copying, recurrence of any kind sustains it.

\textbf{Implications.} In small models trained from scratch, tied or untied, probes with invented words can measure an unseen token's embedding more than anything learned about context; in Pythia this did not hold. Natural text resembles changing-shape bursts in one respect. In WikiText-103 \citep{merity2017}, the 44 nouns of the pretrained test recurred within 128 words after a median 40\% (colour-typical) and 24\% (colour-variable) of their mentions, over 100 times more often than if spread evenly. A colour named before a noun twice in one article was usually the same (median agreement 0.98 and 0.86), whereas two mentions from anywhere agreed less often (0.46 and 0.37; Appendix~\ref{app:O}). Many such pairs are names (\emph{Black Sea}), and our models' copying reached only the next sentence, so this is suggestive only.

\textbf{Analogies with children.} Two findings echo children's word learning, as analogies only. Changing-shape bursts resemble the boxes of \citet{dewar2010}, uniform within and varying between; after one draw from a new box, 9-month-olds expected the next to match, an overhypothesis in the sense of \citet{kemp2007}. And spaced nouns first stored their shape better, while bursts kept the context in use; children retain words better from spaced exposures \citep{childers2002,vlach2008} but learn labels repeated in successive sentences \citep{schwab2016}.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three features and five sentence frames; the models are small (at most 85.6M parameters) and trained with one recipe, and most effects shrank from 3.4M to 25.6M parameters. Experiments~2 and~3 tested 100 and 1,000 exposures, and over training exposure is confounded with training time. The learned copying almost vanished beyond one sentence, transferred only partly to another frame, and did not apply to colour. No-shape comparisons are confounded with expected word class, and bursts weakened stored shapes at 100 exposures. The test of memory competition rests on spaced nouns, which copied little. Single-history corpora used five seeds. The tied-embedding check used one size, and it and the mechanism analyses were added after the results. The mechanism is located only coarsely; the heads involved were not identified, and a registered search for induction-like heads in Experiment~1 found none. Planned power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs and colour only. Earlier experiments in this language, not reported here, were registered in stages (Appendix~\ref{app:reg}).
