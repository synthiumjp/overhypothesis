\title{Same Sentences, Different Order: What the Arrangement of\\Training Text Teaches Small Transformers}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Training pipelines decide how text is arranged when they pack, order or generate it, but the effect of arrangement is rarely tested with content held fixed. We trained small transformers on artificial languages in which the same sentences about 32 kinds of object came in different orders. We then described one object of a new kind and measured whether the models expected a second object of that kind to keep its shape more than its colour. Trained on random order, they expected the shape to change; trained on the same sentences grouped by kind, they expected it to stay. Across conditions their expectations tracked what the next sentence usually does, though not the expectation of change in random order, and two things mattered: a feature fixed within kinds, and nearby sentences about different objects rather than the same object again. These results use test nouns the models had never seen. With nouns from training whose kind always had one shape, a shape given in the context was not carried to the next mention; when a kind's shape changed from one pass to the next, the models carried it forward, but only after the same noun. Models trained on one short fixed file, each position read 160 to 290 times, memorised it; the grouping effect was then about half as large, and the random-order index went from reliably negative to slightly positive. In these small models, what is placed near what in training data, not only what is included, decides what they generalise.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Every training pipeline arranges its text. It packs documents into windows, orders them and, more and more, generates synthetic text whose layout someone chooses \citep{shi2024,zhao2024}. Work on training data mostly asks about content \citep{warstadt2023,feng2024}. Arrangement alone, with content held fixed, is rarely tested, although natural text differs in it: a word that has appeared once in a document is far more likely to appear again soon than its frequency predicts \citep{church2000}, and talk to toddlers returns to the same objects in clusters \citep{slone2023}. In transformers, burstiness of this kind decides whether in-context learning emerges \citep{chan2022}. We ask a smaller question. With the same sentences, what does arrangement teach a model to expect about the next mention of a kind, and which properties of an arrangement matter?

We need a regularity that holds across items, so that arrangement can make it easier or harder to see. We borrow one from word learning: things with the same name tend to share a shape \citep{landau1988,kemp2007}. We give a model one sentence about an object of a new kind, \emph{A snerg is a krannish pulek skorli thing}, where the three adjectives name a shape, a colour and a texture. We then ask how much more it expects a second snerg to keep the shape than the colour. We use this only as a probe of what transformers learn from their data; our claims are about transformers, not children.

A model can meet this probe in two ways. It can carry features forward from the previous sentence, whatever its noun, or it can use what it has stored about a noun. We separate the two by testing with novel nouns, about which nothing is stored, and with nouns from training.

We trained 510 small transformers (3.4M to 25.6M parameters) on artificial languages whose content and order we control. Our main models saw every pass of their corpus arranged afresh; others were trained, as is common, on one fixed file. We find:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Arrangement teaches what the next sentence does.} With novel nouns (tokens the models had never seen), random order taught the models to expect a shape to change and grouping by kind taught them to expect it to stay. Across conditions their expectations tracked a simple learner that predicts each feature from the previous sentence, although it does not explain the expectation of change.
\item \textbf{Two things mattered.} The gain needed a feature fixed within kinds, and nearby sentences about different objects of a kind taught it more selectively than the same object described again.
\item \textbf{Trained nouns took up the context's shape only when their kind's shape changed.} With a noun from training whose kind always had one shape, a shape given in the context was not carried to the next mention (exploratory). When a kind's shape changed between passes, the models carried it forward, but only after the same noun.
\item \textbf{Heavy repetition changes the result.} Models trained on one fixed file, each position read 160 to 290 times, memorised it; the grouping effect was about half as large, and the random-order index was no longer negative.
\end{enumerate}
We also measured the same arrangement statistic in natural corpora and probed pretrained models (Section~\ref{sec:natural}). The confirmatory tests were registered in stages; some registrations were written after earlier results were known, and Appendix~\ref{app:A} gives the dated record.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Arrangement and order.} Packing related documents into one window improves how pretrained models use their context \citep{shi2024,zhao2024}. Curricula that order examples by difficulty have given mixed results \citep{bengio2009,rohde1999,diehlmartinez2023}. At child scale, the local order of child-directed speech affected what models learned, while its global order by age did not \citep{feng2024}. We hold content fixed and vary only local arrangement.

\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} \citet{chan2022} found that in-context learning emerged when items of a class appeared in bursts, more strongly when item meanings were dynamic rather than fixed, and traded off against learning stored in the weights; \citet{singh2023} showed that it can fade as the stored solution takes over, and \citet{reddy2024} traced its onset to induction heads. We study the same trade-off in next-word prediction over text, with the content held fixed and only the arrangement varied.

\textbf{Artificial languages and repeated data.} Artificial languages let a model's preferences be traced to its input \citep{mccoy2020,white2021,kallini2024}. Such studies typically reuse one generated corpus over many passes, from about two to a few dozen in these examples \citep{mccoy2020,kallini2024,murty2023}. The number of passes matters: training long on fixed data changed hierarchical generalisation \citep{murty2023}; at a fixed budget, small sets of repeated examples beat larger sets of single-use examples on synthetic maths tasks \citep{charton2024}; and at scale up to about four epochs of repeated data changed loss negligibly \citep{muennighoff2023}. The BabyLM Challenge capped training at ten epochs in 2025, judging tens or hundreds of repeated exposures developmentally implausible \citep{charpentier2025}.

\textbf{Shape bias in networks.} Networks trained on images or artificial objects can show a shape bias \citep{ritter2017,feinman2018}, and a hierarchical Bayesian model learns it from how kinds vary \citep{kemp2007}. We use it as a probe, not as a target.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Corpora and arrangements}
Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by a novel noun (\emph{wug}, \emph{dax}, \ldots), with 14 exemplars per kind. Each exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each a novel word from a list of ten, and is described by one sentence in one of five frames (Figure~\ref{fig:1}; Appendix~\ref{app:I}). Shape is named in all five frames, colour in three and texture in one. The frames are the same in every corpus, so every conclusion rests on comparisons between corpora. The 448 sentences of a corpus (1,664 for Objects), each used once, make up one pass. Table~\ref{tab:terms} defines the corpora, the arrangements and the measures.

\textbf{Arrangement.} In random order every pass is shuffled. In grouped order the sentences about each kind are cut into runs of four and the runs are shuffled across kinds; every sentence occurs as often as in random order. Graded orders keep each run with a set probability and otherwise split it, giving intermediate clustering. We measure clustering with one statistic, $B$, used for natural corpora too: among consecutive mentions of object nouns no more than 100 words apart, the share that name the same kind, divided by the same share after the sentences of each document (here, each pass) are shuffled. $B = 1$ means no clustering beyond what the content predicts.

\textbf{Fresh and fixed text.} Our main models were trained on fresh text: 7,500 passes of a corpus (2,100 for Objects), each arranged with its own random draw, 34 to 35 million tokens in all, read in non-overlapping 128-token windows so that no window was read twice. A second set of models was trained on one fixed file of 11,648 sentences (about 120,000 tokens) with windows at random positions, so that each position was read 160 to 290 times. Colour stable corpora, three of the six levels of clustering and several controls exist only as fixed files (Table~\ref{tab:1}, Appendix~\ref{app:I}).

\subsection{Models and test}\label{sec:test}
We trained decoder-only transformers of 3.4, 11.0 and 25.6 million parameters (4 to 8 layers) for 5,000 to 10,000 steps, with five seeds per condition at each size; a seed sets the corpus, the weights and the batch order (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). Every artificial word is one token.

Each test item gives the model one frame-1 sentence about an exemplar, then two continuations that differ in one word: one repeats the probed feature and one does not (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). $\Delta$ is the difference in summed log probability between them. Trained models generally prefer words they have just seen, so we report the index, $\Delta_{\text{shape}} - \Delta_{\text{colour}}$, which cancels copying that affects both features equally, together with $\Delta$ for each feature. With one exemplar, nothing in the context shows which feature is fixed, so differences between conditions come from training. Untrained models give an index of 0.02 nats.

Test nouns are of two kinds. \textbf{Novel nouns} never occur in training. Their input embeddings were never trained, because a tie between input and output embeddings was lost on the training hardware, and the models showed no sign of recognising a repeated novel noun (Appendix~\ref{app:E}). \textbf{Trained nouns} occur in training. To keep items comparable, an item never offers a trained noun its own kind's fixed feature, so in Regular corpora both continuations give the noun a shape it never had (Appendix~\ref{app:F}). Each item is also given with a different noun in the continuations; the \textbf{noun-specific index} is the index after the same noun minus the index after a different noun.

\textbf{Statistics.} Models of three sizes with the same seed share a corpus, so the five seeds are the units of replication. We average sizes within each seed and compare conditions by paired $t$ tests over the five seed means (4 degrees of freedom); estimates and 95\% intervals in the text are these seed-level values. Registered contrasts were one-sided with \citet{holm1979} correction within each registration. For fresh-text models the registered test was the seed-level test; for fixed-file models it was a linear mixed model or a Welch test over models, with seed-level results beside them in Appendix~\ref{app:E}. Results given with a Holm $p$ were registered; others are exploratory. Every contrast reported as reliable had the same direction at all three model sizes. Table~\ref{tab:main} lists the main registered tests and marks those written after related results were known.

\subsection{Arrangement teaches what the next sentence does}\label{sec:persist}
With novel nouns, Regular models trained in random order came to expect the shape to \emph{change}: their index was \textminus{}1.56 nats [\textminus{}2.45,~\textminus{}0.67], below zero in all 15 models (Table~\ref{tab:2}). The same sentences in grouped order gave 9.52, higher in all five seeds and at every size (difference 11.08 [9.57,~12.59], Holm $p<.001$). At $B = 3.5$, near the clustering of object labels in speech to children, the index was 1.28, above random order (2.84 [1.41,~4.27], Holm $p=.005$), but small and variable across sizes (1.37, 2.27 and 0.21).

These expectations follow the corpora. In grouped order the next sentence is about the same kind 72\% of the time and then always repeats the shape; in random order it is about the same kind 3\% of the time. A learner that predicts each feature from the previous sentence alone, ignoring the noun, gives a reference index, and the models tracked it across conditions (Pearson $r = .88$ over the eight fresh-text conditions, .95 over 12 fixed-file conditions; exploratory and descriptive, since conditions share seeds, and driven mainly by grouped against ungrouped orders; Appendix~\ref{app:E}). The models also learned how long runs last: with two or four sentences about the new kind in the context, the grouped Regular index fell from 9.52 to 7.88 and 4.24, because after four sentences about a kind the next is about the same kind only 3\% of the time.

The gain needed a fixed feature. When nothing was fixed within kinds, grouped models expected the shape to change, at \textminus{}3.98 (difference from grouped Regular 13.50 [11.48,~15.52], Holm $p<.001$). Two results come only from fixed-file models. When colour was fixed instead, the preference reversed: grouped Colour stable models favoured colour, at \textminus{}3.20, below fixed-file No stable feature models at \textminus{}0.44 (difference 2.76 [1.99,~3.52], Holm $p<.001$). Across six levels of clustering the index rose with $\log B$ (slope 1.63 [1.46,~1.80] nats per log unit, Holm $p<.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:2}).

The reference does not explain two values: it is near zero for random order (\textminus{}0.01) and for No stable feature corpora (0.02), where the models gave \textminus{}1.56 and \textminus{}3.98. We have no tested account of these expectations of change. Both arise when the test noun carries no stored shape (Section~\ref{sec:noun}).

\subsection{Different objects, not the same object again}
At matched clustering, runs of different objects gave a larger index than runs describing one object four times: 7.60 against 5.87 (difference 1.72 [0.54,~2.90], Holm $p=.008$; higher in all five seeds; fixed-file models agreed, 5.65 against 2.99). The components show why. After same-object runs the models expected colour to repeat more ($\Delta$ colour 0.99 against 0.46), as it does in those runs. Their index stayed positive because they also repeated shape, which the frames name more often, more than the corpus warrants; part of this may be copying. Runs of different objects show what varies within a kind and what does not, and taught the fixed feature more selectively. The noun-blind reference predicts this direction, with a larger gap (0.39 against 3.43; Appendix~\ref{app:E}), so the contrast is what the corpora warrant rather than a further property of the models.

\subsection{Trained nouns and the context}\label{sec:noun}
Trained nouns behaved differently (Table~\ref{tab:2}, right; exploratory). In every fresh-text corpus where a kind keeps one shape throughout (Regular and Objects), models tested with a trained noun showed almost no preference about shape in any arrangement: $\Delta$ shape was \textminus{}0.09 to 0.06 after the same noun, against \textminus{}1.55 to 8.05 with novel nouns (grouped Regular: 0.06 [\textminus{}0.27,~0.39] against 6.88 [5.22,~8.54]). So a shape given in the context, never the noun's own, was not carried to the next mention. This is consistent with the models predicting a trained kind's shape from its noun, but our items cannot test that, because they never offer the stored shape. Arrangement still shaped what the noun does not predict: in grouped Regular corpora, trained-noun items showed an expectation that colour would change ($\Delta$ colour \textminus{}1.94 [\textminus{}2.87,~\textminus{}1.02]). In No stable feature corpora, trained nouns, like novel ones, gave a negative $\Delta$ shape after the same noun (\textminus{}3.58 against \textminus{}3.88), but much less after a different noun (\textminus{}1.18).

Reassigned corpora remove the stored shape while keeping the run structure: a kind's shape changes from pass to pass, so only the previous sentence about the kind shows which shape comes next. Trained on them in grouped order, models tested with trained nouns carried the shape forward after the same noun ($\Delta$ shape 10.01) and not after a different one (0.08). Their noun-specific index was 11.24 [10.44,~12.03] (Holm $p<.001$), close to the 11.4 that the corpus warrants. With novel nouns, which the models did not track, the noun-specific index was small (0.37 [0.24,~0.49], Holm $p=.001$). In random order, Reassigned corpora taught neither a preference for shape (0.21) nor a dependence on the noun (\textminus{}0.07 with trained nouns), so the effect needed sentences about a kind to be close together.

So with trained nouns, the context's shape was carried forward only in Reassigned corpora, where the noun could not predict it, and there only after the same noun. This resembles the trade-off between in-context and in-weights learning that \citet{chan2022} found with images, here in text with the content held fixed.

\subsection{Fixed files memorise and change the result}
Fixed-file models memorised their files: their loss was 0.08 to 0.11 nats per token on their own file but 0.66 to 1.26 on a new arrangement of the same sentences, while fresh-text models scored 0.28 to 0.55 on both (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). The arrangement effects changed with it. The grouping effect was about half the size (6.02 [5.24,~6.79] against 11.08), and in random order the index was 0.47 rather than \textminus{}1.56, though not reliably above zero (Holm $p=.061$). Early in training, while their loss was near that of fresh-text models, fixed-file random-order models also gave \textminus{}0.4 to \textminus{}1.5 nats; their index rose above zero only late, as loss fell to the memorised level (exploratory). Every contrast measured in both regimes had the same direction. Their sizes differed, and the random-order index alone would have suggested no expectation, or a weak preference for shape, rather than an expectation that shape changes (fresh minus fixed, \textminus{}2.03 [\textminus{}2.42,~\textminus{}1.65]; exploratory).

Two cautions limit this result. Our fixed files were read 160 to 290 times, more than most artificial-language studies use, although BabyLM entries have trained for hundreds to thousands of epochs \citep{warstadt2023}. And fixed against fresh text changes both the number of passes and the variety of windows, so we cannot say which matters. The result adds to evidence that repeated passes over fixed data change what is learned \citep{murty2023,charton2024}: here they changed the measured effect of arrangement itself.

\section{Natural text and pretrained models}
\label{sec:natural}
\textbf{Natural corpora.} With the same statistic, we measured how clustered mentions of 70 object nouns are in speech recorded around children under three (CHILDES; \citealp{macwhinney2000}), adult conversation (CABank) and written text (the Pile; \citealp{gao2020}). Labels were clustered in all three, most in speech to children ($B$ = 3.57 [3.13,~4.30]; above written text, Holm $p<.001$; Table~\ref{tab:natural}). But a repeated label need not bring a new object: in 500 hand-coded pairs, nearly half of the repetitions in speech to children returned to the same object, and a quarter brought a different one. Counting only different objects, effective clustering was 1.95, 1.66 and 1.22, which places all three corpora between random order and $B = 3.6$ on our scale, where the fixed-file index rose only from 0.47 to 1.43 nats (Figure~\ref{fig:2}). Speech to children differed more in density, with about 2.8 nearby different-object repetitions per 1,000 words against 0.1 to 0.2 elsewhere (exploratory; partly a result of our choice of nouns). Appendix~\ref{app:J} gives the method and checks.

\textbf{Pretrained models.} On English versions of the items, the index of Pythia models \citep{biderman2023} rose with size ($\rho = .92$, Holm $p=.003$); on items whose two words have the same number of tokens (exploratory), every model from 1B parameters was above zero (from 1.00 nats at 1B to 1.62 at 12B). It did not rise steadily over training: at every size it first fell to about \textminus{}2 nats, as in our random-order models, and only larger models recovered. No Pythia model, nor Qwen2.5 72B or Llama 3.1 70B, preferred shape over texture, and evidence of a noun-tied expectation was weak and depended on wording (Appendix~\ref{app:K}). We did not arrange their data, so this is a comparison, not a test.

\section{Discussion}
Small transformers learned what the next sentence usually does. That much is expected of next-word prediction. What the controlled arrangement adds is which properties of the stream matter and how the stream interacts with what a model has stored. A regularity across items was taught by grouping only when a feature was fixed within kinds, and more selectively by nearby sentences about different objects than by the same object described again. With trained nouns, the models carried a shape forward from the context only when the noun could not predict it, and then only after the same noun, which resembles the trade-off between in-context and in-weights learning \citep{chan2022,singh2023}. We identify the training conditions that produce these behaviours, not their mechanism; carrying a word forward is the kind of behaviour attributed to induction heads \citep{olsson2022}, but we did not examine attention. People show related patterns: blocked study helps learners find what members of a category share \citep{carvalho2014}, and varied exemplars taught toddlers more than near-identical ones \citep{perry2010}. We note the parallel without taking our results as evidence about how children learn.

Two practical points follow for small models, with the caveat that we have not tested larger ones. First, arrangement is a lever separate from content: to teach a regularity that holds across instances, place different instances of a kind near each other. Second, small-scale experiments on data should report how many passes they make over fixed text; arranging each pass afresh removes memorisation at no cost in content.

\section*{Limitations}
The artificial language has 32 kinds, three dimensions and five frames that name shape more often than colour or texture; conclusions rest on comparisons between corpora that share the frames. The models are small (3.4M to 25.6M parameters), trained with one recipe and five seeds per condition, and we do not know how the effects scale. Colour stable corpora and half of the levels of clustering exist only as fixed files, which their models memorised. Fixed and fresh text differ in both the number of passes and the variety of windows, and our fixed files were read more often than in most published studies. Novel test nouns had untrained input embeddings, so the models could not recognise them as the same kind. Trained-noun items never offer the noun's own shape, so they show that the context's shape was ignored, not how the stored shape is used, and the comparison of trained with novel nouns is exploratory. The negative indices in random order and in No stable feature corpora are unexplained. The clustering statistic counts repeated labels, not referents, and effective clustering rests on 500 pairs coded by one person, with a language model as a consistency check. All corpora are English. The registrations were made in stages, several after earlier results were known, and a loader error, corrected before the final analyses, had changed two verdicts. The analysis of pretrained models does not manipulate their training data.
