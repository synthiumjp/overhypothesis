\title{Whether Small Transformers Copy a Feature from Context\\Depends on the Noun's Training History}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A language model can predict a word's properties by copying them from context or recalling what it stored. We ask what in a word's training history decides which. We trained 150 small transformers on an artificial language about objects, controlling how often 40 new nouns occurred, with which shapes, and whether their sentences came one at a time or in bursts. Copying is how much more probable (in nats) the model made a shape named in the preceding sentence than other shapes. Never-seen nouns copied, but mainly because their input embeddings were untrained. In corpora where object kinds keep their shapes, nouns met one sentence at a time lost most of their copying with exposure; a stored shape reduced copying little but outweighed the copied shape (exploratory). Nouns met in bursts that shared a shape, with the shape changing between bursts, kept copying: 3.4 nats more than the same sentences spaced out (pre-registered), and 2.9 nats more after 1,000 sentences than bursts with a fixed shape (exploratory). This copying was as strong when the preceding sentence described another noun, and when shapes changed across the corpus, all bursts kept copying (exploratory). In Pythia, replacing nouns' embeddings had no consistent effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a language model reads \emph{The car was red. The car was}, it can predict \emph{red} because the context says so, or predict a typical car colour from what it has stored. Which source it relies on matters for how models handle documents that contradict their knowledge \citep{longpre2021,xie2024}, for how they learn new words and tokens, and for how we interpret probes that use invented words. Work on in-context and in-weights learning shows that the balance depends on the training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning from context; common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights (\citealp{chan2022}; \citealp{chan2025}). Inside pretrained models, mechanisms that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask the same question about a single word: \emph{what in a word's own training history decides whether a model copies its features from the context?}

Three accounts are plausible, and they make different predictions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Memory competition.} A model copies until it has stored something about the word; stored knowledge then competes with the context. Words with a stored feature should stop copying sooner than words without one.
\item \textbf{Representation.} Copying for new words reflects their unusual, untrained embeddings. Once a word's embedding looks like those of familiar words, copying should stop, whatever the word was seen with.
\item \textbf{Useful context.} A model learns, word by word, whether copying has paid off. A word should keep being copied if, in training, its own earlier mentions predicted its features and memory could not.
\end{itemize}
Natural data cannot separate these, because frequent words are both familiar and well known. Invented (nonce) words, the usual probe \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}, are new in both respects at once, and conclusions drawn with them can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}.

We therefore built the separation into training. Small transformers learned an artificial language describing objects by shape, colour and texture, into which we inserted 40 new nouns. For each noun we set how often it occurred, which shapes it occurred with, and whether its sentences came one at a time or in short bursts (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). We measured how strongly each model copied a shape named in one sentence into the next mention of a noun. A first pre-registered experiment tested the memory and representation accounts. A second, also pre-registered, removed two differences between new nouns and the language's own nouns that the first left open, and tested the useful-context account. Last, we asked whether the effect of a word's embedding carries over to pretrained models.

In corpora where object kinds keep their shapes, the results favour a combination: copying starts high because new nouns' embeddings are untrained, and is retained only where the noun's own context was useful. New nouns copied mainly because of their untrained embeddings. Nouns met one sentence at a time lost most of their copying, whether or not they had a stored shape. Nouns whose earlier mentions had been the sole source of their current shape kept copying. What they kept was a tendency, stored in the noun's embedding, to copy the latest shape, not a link between the noun and its shape. When the corpus as a whole made shapes changeable, bursts of any kind kept copying. Our claims are about small transformers trained on this language; we note parallels with children's word learning in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}; it depends on task diversity \citep{raventos2023}, favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023}. Its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}, and in small transformers stored bigram statistics are learned quickly and in-context ones later, through an induction head \citep{bietti2023}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. We manipulate frequency, stored knowledge and arrangement separately for single words within one model.

\textbf{Copying and recall.} Language models have long gained from copying recent words, through caches and pointers \citep{kuhn1990,grave2017,merity2017}, because words recur in bursts: a word that has occurred once is far more likely to occur again \citep{church1995,church2000}, a regularity that human memory also tracks \citep{anderson1991}. Pretrained models answer from memory more for frequent entities \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023,razeghi2022} and resist contexts that contradict what they know \citep{longpre2021,xie2024,du2024}; copying and recall compete inside the network \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}.

\textbf{New words and new tokens.} Rare and new words have poor representations \citep{schick2020}, under-trained tokens behave abnormally \citep{land2024}, and new embeddings are often initialised at the mean of existing ones \citep{hewitt2021}. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and when language models acquire a word depends mainly on its frequency \citep{chang2022}. Children's word learning offers related evidence on shape \citep{landau1988,smith2002}, on expectations about how features vary \citep{kemp2007,dewar2010} and on the timing of exposures \citep{childers2002,schwab2016}, to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by an invented noun (the \emph{trained kinds}), with 14 exemplars per kind. An exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten invented words, and is described in one of five sentence frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture) or \emph{Look at the wug. It is zibby and lavo} (Appendix~\ref{app:I}). The corpus runs through all exemplars many times, each pass arranged afresh, and a model reads the corpus once. In \textbf{stable-shape} corpora each kind keeps one shape, so a kind's shape can be stored. In \textbf{shifting-shape} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass and keeps it within the pass, so its current shape can come only from the context. In both, sentences about a kind come in runs of four; a control corpus uses random order. Every word is one token.

\textbf{Models.} Decoder-only transformers with pre-layer normalisation and learned position embeddings, with 3.4M, 11.0M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers), read the text once in non-overlapping windows of 128 tokens. Input and output embeddings are untied, so a noun's input embedding (its \emph{row}) carries everything the model has learned about the noun as an input. We trained ten seeds of each size (Appendix~\ref{app:C}).

\textbf{Controlling a noun's history.} We added 40 new nouns to each corpus. Four were never used in training (\emph{unseen}); the others are \emph{familiar} in the sense that they occur in training. Each familiar noun had one history (Table~\ref{tab:design}) at a set number of exposures, counted in sentences, with three nouns per condition, rotated over nouns across seeds. A noun's sentences were spread evenly from 5\% to 95\% of training, and each lay wholly inside one window. Presented \emph{spaced}, a noun has one sentence per window. Presented in \emph{bursts}, it has four consecutive sentences in one window, so the same number of sentences occupies a quarter as many windows.

\textbf{Two experiments.} Experiment~1 (90 models) crossed fixed-, changing- and no-shape nouns, all spaced, with 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, in stable-shape, shifting-shape and random-order corpora. Fixed- and changing-shape nouns appeared in all five frames, and no-shape nouns in a frame that names no shape (\emph{The \textit{noun} is \textit{colour} and \textit{texture}}). In that experiment familiar nouns never recurred within a window, unlike the trained kinds, and no-shape nouns never appeared in the frame used for testing. Experiment~2 (60 models) removed both differences. It crossed the three histories with spaced and burst presentation at 100 and 1,000 exposures, in stable-shape and shifting-shape corpora. Fixed- and changing-shape nouns appeared only in the test frame, and no-shape nouns in that frame without the shape (\emph{A \textit{noun} is a \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}). This frame has a side effect: no-shape nouns learn to expect a colour word, not a shape, after \emph{is a}, by 1 to 6 nats at the test position, whereas every other familiar noun expects a shape (Appendix~\ref{app:N}). Comparisons involving no-shape nouns in Experiment~2 are therefore confounded with expected word class. The experiments also differ in how much of the text the new nouns fill: about 60\% of training windows in Experiment~1 and 8\% in Experiment~2 (39\% and 5\% for the 11.0M models, which read more windows). We therefore compare conditions only within an experiment.

\textbf{Measures.} A test item gives one sentence about a noun naming a shape $s$, then the start of the next mention, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the model's log probability $L$ of each of the ten shape words at the next position (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). The \textbf{copy score} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes, leaving out the noun's own shape (a fixed placebo shape for nouns that have none), which $s$ never is. It is in nats: 0 means the context's shape is no more likely than the others, and 3.4 means it is about 30 times as likely as the (geometric) average other shape. Each noun gets 36 items, four for each shape other than its own, with colours and textures drawn at random. In a \textbf{control}, the query names another new noun with the same history; if copying is tied to the noun, the control should score lower. \textbf{Knowledge} is $L$ of the noun's own shape minus the mean $L$ of the other nine after an unrelated sentence, also in nats. We also score unseen nouns with a \textbf{typical embedding}: the mean of the trained kinds' rows plus a random offset whose length is the trained rows' average distance from that mean.

\textbf{Predictions.} Table~\ref{tab:pred} gives what each account predicts for the comparisons that separate them. The memory account alone predicts that a stored shape lowers copying. The useful-context account alone predicts that changing-shape bursts copy more than changing-shape spaced nouns, unless bursts also keep rows less trained, which the representation account would then predict too. Comparing changing- with fixed-shape bursts, which have the same number of updates, separates useful context from the representation account. The first of these comparisons was pre-registered; the other two were exploratory.

\textbf{Analysis and registration.} The seed is the unit of replication: we average each condition over the three model sizes within a seed and test across ten seeds, with Holm correction within each experiment \citep{holm1979}, and count a prediction as supported only if its direction also holds in two of the three sizes. \section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{New nouns copy because their embeddings are untrained}\label{sec:rows}
In stable-shape models of Experiment~1, unseen nouns had a copy score of 4.53 nats [95\% CI 3.54,~5.52]. Given a typical embedding, the same nouns scored $-$0.33 (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left; exploratory). Earlier experiments with this language had shown the same drop with a related measure (Appendix~\ref{app:L}). To see which property of an untrained row matters, we changed only the rows of unseen nouns in the Experiment~1 models. Scaling a row to the trained kinds' average size, keeping its direction, lowered the score only from 4.53 to 4.00 (pre-registered; almost entirely in the smallest models). A random direction of that size kept copying (4.09), whereas the trained kinds' mean row removed it ($-$0.44; both exploratory). What suppresses copying is therefore what a row shares with trained nouns' rows, not its size. In shifting-shape models, scaling raised the score instead (exploratory). In these small models, then, a probe with an unseen word measures the word's untrained row as much as anything learned about context.

\subsection{Nouns met one at a time lose copying, whatever they store}
Experiment~1 tested the memory and representation accounts. Changing-shape nouns, which never have a shape to store, are the cleanest test of the memory account. In stable-shape models they lost copying almost as quickly as fixed-shape nouns, which did store their shape (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left): the two stayed within 0.4 nats of each other at every exposure, although from 1,000 exposures on fixed-shape nouns copied reliably less (by 0.30 nats [0.23,~0.38] at 1,000 and 0.35 [0.28,~0.42] at 10,000; exploratory). Fixed-shape nouns learned their shape well, preferring it by 8.3 to 9.5 nats after an unrelated sentence at 1,000 exposures (pre-registered manipulation check), yet their copy score did not fall faster than that of no-shape nouns (pre-registered prediction, not supported). For all three histories, copying fell from 3.6--4.5 nats at 10 exposures to under 1 nat at 100 and to near zero from 1,000 on. No-shape nouns at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures ended 0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52] above unseen nouns with a typical embedding, a reliable difference that was nonetheless within the registered 1-nat equivalence bound (pre-registered). In random-order corpora no noun copied (exploratory), so copying is learned from the runs of sentences about a kind.

These results give the memory account little support, a stored shape lowering copying by at most 0.35 nats, and fit the representation account: once a noun was familiar, it lost copying largely whatever it had stored. In shifting-shape corpora, familiar nouns kept more copying (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, middle; Section~\ref{sec:shifting}). Experiment~2 asked whether recurrence, or a context that is useful, keeps copying alive in stable-shape corpora.

\subsection{Bursts with changing shapes keep copying}
The useful-context account predicts that changing-shape bursts, where a noun's previous sentence gives its current shape and memory cannot, keep copying (Table~\ref{tab:pred}); the other two accounts predict no difference from spaced presentation. Changing-shape bursts scored 3.39 nats [2.69,~4.09] more than changing-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered; Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, left; Table~\ref{tab:key}), as much at 1,000 exposures (3.35) as at 100 (3.43). After 1,000 exposures they still scored 3.85 nats, against about 0.5 to 1 for every other familiar history and 0.61 for unseen nouns with a typical embedding. Final models copied somewhat less at 1,000 exposures than at 100 (3.85 against 5.02), but over training this copying did not fade: for nouns with 1,000 exposures, changing-shape bursts copied 0.8 to 2.6 nats at the first saved weights, depending on model size, and 3.85 at the end, while every other history stayed below 2 nats and ended near or below 1 (exploratory; Figure~\ref{fig:a10formation}). Exposure and training time rise together here.

Because bursts occupy fewer windows, a burst noun's row is updated less often, which might leave it closer to an untrained row. Fixed-shape bursts control for this: the same frame, presentation and number of windows, but a shape that never changes, so memory can supply it. Changing-shape bursts scored 1.73 nats [1.25,~2.20] more than fixed-shape bursts, and 2.86 [1.99,~3.74] more at 1,000 exposures (exploratory). This comparison separates useful context from the representation account (Table~\ref{tab:pred}); it was exploratory, and it was largest in the smallest models (Section~\ref{sec:scale}). Section~\ref{sec:rowsexp2} examines the rows directly.

Recurrence without a useful context helped less and only early. No-shape bursts scored 0.80 nats [0.47,~1.13] more than no-shape spaced nouns, which we had registered as evidence that recurrence preserves copying (pre-registered). Split by exposure (exploratory), the gain was 1.55 nats at 100 exposures and 0.05 [$-$0.30,~0.40] at 1,000. Fixed-shape bursts scored 1.69 nats [1.29,~2.08] more than fixed-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered), but this difference cannot be credited to recurrence: at 100 exposures, bursts also left the stored shape weaker (knowledge 3.0 against 5.4 nats), and the registered check that knowledge be equal failed. At 1,000 exposures, where knowledge was equal, bursts added 0.47 nats [0.27,~0.66] (exploratory), about a sixth of the advantage of changing-shape bursts.

\subsection{A stored shape adds to copying rather than suppressing it}
Fixed- and changing-shape nouns presented spaced share frame, presentation and expected word class, and differ only in whether one shape can be stored. Their copy scores were the same: $-$0.02 nats [$-$0.16,~0.11], equivalent to zero within $\pm$0.3 nats (exploratory, TOST \citealp{lakens2017}, $p<.001$), and $-$0.07 [$-$0.29,~0.15] at 100 exposures, where both still copied about 1.5 nats. The copy score compares the context's shape with shapes the noun has not stored, so it measures the copying itself. Which shape the model predicts is another matter: fixed-shape spaced nouns still preferred their own shape to the context's by 4.5 nats at 100 exposures and 7.7 at 1,000 (exploratory). The stored shape was thus added to the copying rather than suppressing it, and in behaviour it won. The registered comparisons with no-shape nouns point the other way but are confounded. Fixed-shape spaced nouns scored 1.02 nats [0.76,~1.27] below no-shape spaced nouns, and no-shape spaced nouns scored 1.42 [1.12,~1.73] above unseen nouns with a typical embedding, not equivalent to them (both pre-registered). Since no-shape nouns expect a colour where the others expect a shape, these differences reflect expected word class, not a stored shape. A stored shape mattered for copying only through the context: fixed-shape bursts, whose earlier sentences predicted a shape that memory also supplied, lost copying, and changing-shape bursts did not.

\subsection{History sets whether the model copies, not what it copies}
If changing-shape bursts taught the model to bind a noun to its current shape, the control, in which the query names another noun, should score lower. It did not. In stable-shape models, the copy score with a context about the queried noun exceeded the control by at most 0.07 nats in any Experiment~2 condition, and 0.14 in Experiment~1 (exploratory). The model copied the most recent shape after the queried noun, whichever noun the preceding sentence described. To see whose history matters, we paired a context about a noun of one history with a query naming a noun of another (24 items per pair and model; exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:N14}). Copying depended mostly on the queried noun: at 1,000 exposures, a queried noun with changing-shape bursts copied 1.35 nats [0.97,~1.74] more than one with fixed-shape bursts, averaged over the context noun's history, whereas the context noun's history added 0.39 [0.19,~0.58]. In training, every sentence of a burst followed one about the same noun, so nothing required the model to check which noun a shape belonged to.

We registered a search for heads that carry the shape: replace with their mean output the heads (two to six per model) that score highest on the induction-head signature of \citet{olsson2022}, measured on repeated random sequences of the language's words as the product of attending to the token after an earlier copy of the current token (prefix matching) and raising that token's probability (copying). No head matched prefixes clearly better than uniform attention (highest score 0.043, against 0.027), so the candidates were first-layer copying heads and the search could not test what it was registered to test. Ablating them lowered unseen nouns' copy score from 4.53 to 2.04 nats on average, against 2.68 for random heads, a difference that was not reliable; the registered prediction that they remove more than half of copying was not supported (Appendix~\ref{app:M}). The mechanism remains open.

\subsection{The copying lives in the noun's row}\label{sec:rowsexp2}
A noun enters the model only through its row, so whatever its history taught must be stored there. The question raised by bursts is whether changing-shape bursts simply leave the row less trained. We examined every familiar noun's row in the Experiment~2 models (exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:N13}). We measured each row's geometry (its cosine with its initial value, its distance from the trained kinds' mean row and its norm) and regressed copy scores on it, with model size and seed. Rows of nouns met in bursts stayed closer to their initial values than rows of spaced nouns, and at 100 exposures geometry accounted for the copying of changing-shape bursts: they copied no more than other histories with the same geometry ($-$0.09 nats [$-$0.51,~0.33]). By 1,000 exposures this no longer held. Changing-shape burst rows were only slightly closer to their initial values than fixed-shape burst rows (cosine 0.85 against 0.78), yet they copied 3.19 nats [2.46,~3.91] more than their geometry predicts. Swapping rows between the two histories moved the copying with the row (0.97 and 3.86 nats), as it must, since each noun is a single token; the regression shows that what moves is not the row's distance from its start. First-layer attention from the query to the context's shape tracked copying across nouns (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.81) and was higher for changing-shape bursts. Bursts thus first preserve copying by leaving rows near their initial state, and changing-shape bursts then give the row a copying tendency of its own.

This learned copying is narrow. With one unrelated sentence between the context and the query, copying almost vanished (at most 0.37 nats, a tenth of the score without the gap). With the context in another frame (\emph{Look at the \textit{noun}. It is \textit{shape} and \textit{colour}.}), changing-shape bursts kept about 40\% of their copying (1.53 nats at 1,000 exposures, 1.21 more than fixed-shape bursts). For colour, which varies freely within every kind, no noun copied.

\subsection{When the whole corpus rewards copying}\label{sec:shifting}
These conclusions hold for stable-shape corpora. In shifting-shape corpora, where the trained kinds' own shapes can be recovered only from context, copying behaved differently (Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, right; all exploratory). Part of it was tied to the noun: a context about the queried noun raised the copy score over the control by 0.48 nats for unseen nouns and 1.45 for changing-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures. Every burst history kept copying, not only changing-shape bursts: at 1,000 exposures, fixed-shape bursts scored 3.57 nats and no-shape bursts 2.09, although bursts again weakened stored shapes. And changing-shape bursts copied more than unseen nouns (6.82 against 5.02). What a noun's history teaches therefore depends on what the rest of the corpus teaches about stored features: when the corpus rewards copying, recurrence alone sustains it.

\subsection{Model size}\label{sec:scale}
Most effects were largest in the smallest models (Table~\ref{tab:N11}). From 3.4M to 25.6M parameters, the advantage of changing-shape bursts over spaced presentation fell from 5.05 to 2.29 nats and over fixed-shape bursts from 2.94 to 1.00 nats, while unseen nouns with a typical embedding copied more (from $-$0.30 to 1.51). The main effects kept their direction at every size, but most shrink as models grow over this range.



\subsection{Pretrained models}\label{sec:pythia}
Finally, we asked whether the untrained-embedding effect carries over to pretrained models. We registered three predictions for Pythia models from 1B to 12B parameters \citep{biderman2023}: that replacing a noun's embedding with a random one of untrained scale would raise copying, more than replacing it with a typical one, and, by the memory account, that nouns with a typical colour would copy less than nouns without one. Each needed support in four of the five models, and changes were counted as zero only if equivalent within $\pm$0.3 nats. We used 22 pairs of one-token nouns, one colour-typical (\emph{sky}, \emph{leaf}, \emph{bone}) and one colour-variable (\emph{truck}, \emph{hat}, \emph{dog}), matched on frequency in the Pile \citep{gao2020,liu2024infinigram} and on count or mass use, in frames such as \emph{The \textit{noun} was \textit{colour}. The \textit{noun} was}. Because real nouns have colour priors, the copy score here compares contexts: the log probability of a colour after a context naming it minus that after a context naming another, averaged over the 11 basic colour words. The models had stored typical colours: with no context, colour-typical nouns preferred their colour by 1.3 to 1.7 nats, and human colour norms \citep{paik2021} agreed with our coding for 15 of the 16 colour-typical nouns they cover (both exploratory).

Real nouns copied 1.9 to 2.7 nats of colour (Figure~\ref{fig:pythia}). None of the predictions held (pre-registered). The random replacement raised copying reliably in two models and lowered it in two others, by up to 0.4 nats; the typical replacement changed it by up to 0.6 nats in either direction; and colour-typical and colour-variable nouns did not differ reliably. Most changes were also not small enough to count as zero, so the result is inconsistent rather than null. Changing only the size of the random replacement changed copying by less than 0.01 nats in the registered models (exploratory). In the 70M and 160M models, outside the registered set, the pattern differed again: at 160M parameters the random replacement doubled copying. The untrained-embedding effect of the small models did not transfer to these models and this probe.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What decides copying.} In stable-shape corpora our results fit a combined account. Whether the model copies the latest shape after a noun depends on the noun's row. The tendency starts high for rows far from the trained nouns' rows, which is why unseen nouns copy and lose it with a typical row. Training removes it, whatever is stored, unless the noun's own earlier sentences were the only source of its current shape: changing-shape bursts kept copying where fixed-shape bursts with the same recurrence did not. The memory account found little support: a stored shape did not speed up the loss of copying, lowered it by at most 0.35 nats, and otherwise added to it rather than suppressing it. Row analyses support this: early on, bursts keep copying by keeping rows near their initial state; later, changing-shape bursts give the row a copying tendency that geometry alone does not explain and that moves with the row. For nouns with 1,000 exposures it kept rising over training.

\textbf{What is expected and what is not.} That a model copies where copying lowers its loss is expected; two things are less so. Recurrence alone did not keep copying in stable-shape corpora: no-shape bursts lost their advantage over spaced presentation by 1,000 exposures. And a stored shape barely suppressed copying: memory and context added up, and the stored shape won by outweighing the copied one. That copying was not tied to the noun fits training in which it never needed to be. Together, this is the population-level finding of \citet{chan2022}, that bursty items whose meaning changes favour learning in context, acting on single words within one model, but only when the rest of the corpus does not already reward copying.

\textbf{Implications.} In small models trained this way, probes with invented words can measure an unseen token's untrained row more than anything learned about context, so effects should be checked with rows that look familiar. Pythia did not show the embedding effect; whether pretrained models' copying depends on a word's history needs measures of each word's burstiness in pretraining.

\textbf{Analogies with children.} Two findings echo research on children's word learning, as analogies only. Changing-shape bursts resemble the boxes of \citet{dewar2010}, uniform within and varying between; after one draw from a new box, 9-month-olds expected the next to match, an overhypothesis in the sense of \citet{kemp2007}. And at 100 exposures spaced nouns stored their shape more strongly than nouns met in bursts, while bursts kept the context in use. Children likewise retain words and categories better from spaced than from massed exposures \citep{childers2002,vlach2008}, although they learn new labels better when the labels are repeated in successive sentences \citep{schwab2016}.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three features and five sentence frames; the models are small and trained with one recipe, and most effects shrank with model size. The comparison that separates useful context from the representation account was exploratory. Experiment~2 tested 100 and 1,000 exposures, and over training exposure is confounded with training time. The learned copying almost vanished beyond one sentence, transferred only partly to another frame, and did not apply to colour. The copy score measures copying, not which shape the model finally predicts. No-shape comparisons in Experiment~2 are confounded with expected word class, and bursts weakened stored shapes at 100 exposures. The other new nouns in a corpus may affect a noun's copying, and we did not train models with a single history. The unseen-noun result depends on untied embeddings, which leave unseen rows untrained. The head search could not test its hypothesis, and the mechanism is unknown. Planned power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57, below the target of 0.8. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs, three frames and colour only. Earlier experiments in this language were registered in stages, some after related results were known (Appendix~\ref{app:A}).

All training data are artificial; the pretrained models are public. No data were collected from people. Code, corpora, results and all trained models will be released; anonymised copies are provided as supplementary material.
