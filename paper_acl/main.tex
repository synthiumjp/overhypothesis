\title{Whether Small Transformers Copy a Feature from Context\\Depends on the Noun's Training History}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A language model can predict a word's properties by copying them from context or recalling them. Across populations of items, bursty items whose meaning changes favour copying; we test this word by word within one model. We trained small transformers on an artificial language, controlling how often new nouns occurred, with which shapes, and whether their sentences came singly or in bursts. Where object kinds keep their shapes, nouns met more often copied a shape from the previous sentence less, except nouns whose own earlier sentences were the only clue to their current shape. In a pre-registered replication, nouns in bursts whose shape varied between bursts copied 3.7 nats more after 1,000 sentences than nouns in fixed-shape bursts (6.4, 2.4 and 2.2 at 3.4M, 10.9M and 25.6M parameters; 4.2 at 85.6M; 1.9 with tied embeddings). Recurrence alone did not keep copying: by 1,000 sentences, bursts whose shape was redrawn every sentence copied no more than spaced nouns. A stored shape barely suppressed the copied one but outweighed it at the output; its larger effect was on what training kept. What bursts kept was mostly a tendency to copy the latest shape, not a link between noun and shape.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a language model reads \emph{The car was red. The car was}, it can predict \emph{red} because the context says so, or predict a typical car colour from what it has stored. Which source it relies on matters for how models handle documents that contradict their knowledge \citep{longpre2021,xie2024}, for how they learn new words and tokens, and for how we interpret probes that use invented words. Work on in-context and in-weights learning shows that the balance depends on the training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning from context; common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights (\citealp{chan2022}; \citealp{chan2025}). Inside pretrained models, mechanisms that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask the same question about a single word: \emph{what in a word's own training history decides whether a model copies its features from the context?}

Three accounts are plausible, and they make different predictions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Memory competition.} When the model predicts, a stored feature competes with the context. Words with a stored feature should copy less than words without one.
\item \textbf{Representation.} New words copy because their embeddings are untrained. Once a word's embedding looks like those of familiar words, copying should stop, whatever the word was seen with.
\item \textbf{Useful context.} During training, the model learns for each word whether copying has paid off. A word should keep being copied if its own earlier mentions predicted its features and memory could not.
\end{itemize}
Natural data cannot separate these, because frequent words are both familiar and well known. Invented (nonce) words, the usual probe \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}, are new in both respects at once, and conclusions drawn with them can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical}.

We therefore built the separation into training. Small transformers learned an artificial language describing objects by shape, colour and texture, into which we inserted 40 new nouns. For each noun we set how often it occurred, which shapes it occurred with, and whether its sentences came one at a time or in short bursts (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). We measured how strongly each model copied a shape named in one sentence into the next mention of a noun. A first experiment set the baseline with nouns met one sentence at a time. A second added bursts, and a third, registered after the second's results, replicated it on new models. A control then separated recurrence from usefulness.

The models learn to copy from the runs of sentences about a kind, and unseen nouns copy because nothing in their untrained embeddings turns this off. Familiar nouns copy less the more often they have occurred, except where a noun's own earlier sentences were the only source of its current shape, as in bursts whose shape changes between bursts. Recurrence alone does not keep it: bursts whose shape is redrawn every sentence lose it like spaced nouns, and bursts that repeat their shape half the time keep part of it. A stored shape barely suppresses the copied one, although it outweighs it at the output; its larger effect is on what training keeps. What these bursts keep is mostly a tendency to copy the latest shape rather than a link between noun and shape, and how much of it a noun keeps depends on the other nouns in the corpus. The pattern is clearest in the smallest models and with untied embeddings, and the copying is narrow: one intervening sentence removes it. Our claims are about small transformers trained on this language; we note parallels with children's word learning in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}; what is learned in context also generalises differently \citep{chan2022generalize}. It depends on task diversity \citep{raventos2023}, favours rare classes \citep{chan2025} and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{singh2023}. Its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}, and in small transformers stored bigram statistics are learned quickly and in-context ones later \citep{bietti2023}. Synthetic languages and Markov chains have been used to trace how in-context learning forms \citep{akyurek2024,edelman2024}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. We manipulate frequency, stored knowledge and arrangement separately for single words within one model.

\textbf{Copying and recall.} Language models have long gained from copying recent words, through caches and pointers \citep{kuhn1990,grave2017,merity2017}, because words recur in bursts \citep{church1995,church2000,madsen2005}, a regularity that human memory also tracks \citep{anderson1991}. Pretrained models answer from memory more for frequent entities \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023,razeghi2022} and resist contexts that contradict what they know \citep{longpre2021,xie2024,du2024}; copying and recall compete inside the network \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}.

\textbf{New words and new tokens.} Rare and new words have poor representations \citep{schick2020}, and good embeddings for them can be built from a few contexts \citep{herbelot2017,khodak2018}; under-trained tokens behave abnormally \citep{land2024}, new embeddings are often initialised at the mean of existing ones \citep{hewitt2021}, and tying input and output embeddings \citep{press2017,inan2017} trains every token's embedding as an output. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and when language models acquire a word depends mainly on its frequency \citep{chang2022}. Children's word learning offers related evidence on shape \citep{landau1988,smith2002}, on expectations about how features vary \citep{kemp2007,dewar2010} and on the timing of exposures \citep{childers2002,schwab2016}, to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by an invented noun (the \emph{trained kinds}), with 14 exemplars per kind. An exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten invented words, and is described in one of five sentence frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture) or \emph{Look at the wug. It is zibby and lavo} (Appendix~\ref{app:lang}). The corpus runs through all exemplars many times, each pass arranged afresh, and a model reads it once. Sentences about a kind come in runs of four. In \textbf{stable-kind} corpora each kind keeps one shape, so its shape can be stored. In \textbf{shifting-kind} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass, so its current shape can come only from the context. Every word is one token.

\textbf{Models.} Decoder-only transformers with pre-layer normalisation and learned position embeddings, with 3.4M, 10.9M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers), and in Experiment~3 also 85.6M (12 layers), read the text once in windows of 128 tokens. All sizes reached a similar final training loss (0.39 to 0.40 nats per token). Sizes also differ in training steps, batch size, learning rate and amount of text (Appendix~\ref{app:train}), so differences between sizes are not a scaling trend. Input and output embeddings are untied, so a noun's input embedding carries everything the model has learned about the noun as an input; we also check tied embeddings.

\textbf{Controlling a noun's history.} We added 40 new nouns to each corpus. Four never occurred in training (\emph{unseen}); the other 36 did (\emph{familiar}). Each familiar noun had one history (Table~\ref{tab:design}) and a set number of sentences (\emph{exposures}), spread evenly from 5\% to 95\% of training, with three nouns per condition. Presented \emph{spaced}, a noun has one sentence per window. Presented in \emph{bursts}, it has four consecutive sentences in one window, like the trained kinds' runs. In \emph{mixed} corpora the familiar nouns are spread over all histories; in \emph{single-history} corpora all have changing shapes (\emph{changing-only}) or all fixed shapes (\emph{fixed-only}).

\textbf{Three experiments} (Table~\ref{tab:exps}). Experiment~1 asked whether familiarity alone ends copying, using spaced nouns from 10 to 10,000 exposures. Its nouns differed from the trained kinds in two ways: they never recurred within a window, and no-shape nouns never appeared in the test frame. Experiment~2 removed both differences and added bursts, at 100 and 1,000 exposures, where Experiment~1's copying had nearly vanished. Its no-shape nouns, now in the test frame without a shape, learned to expect a colour after \emph{is a} (by 1 to 5 nats), so comparisons with them are confounded with word class. Experiment~3 tested Experiment~2's findings on new models: ten new seeds at the three sizes, ten models of 85.6M parameters, and five seeds in single-history corpora. A \textbf{control}, added after review and not pre-registered, but with its design and predictions fixed before training, kept Experiment~3's seeds and sizes and added \emph{redrawn-shape bursts}, whose shape is drawn anew for every sentence (the sentences of changing-shape spaced nouns, grouped in fours), and \emph{half-kept bursts}, in which each sentence keeps the previous shape with probability one half. New nouns fill about 60\% of training windows in Experiment~1 and 8\% in Experiments~2 and~3 (40\% and 5\% for the 10.9M models), so we compare conditions only within an experiment.

\textbf{Measures.} A test item gives one sentence about a noun naming a shape $s$, then the start of the next mention, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the model's log probability $L$ of each of the ten shapes at the next position (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). The \textbf{copy score} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes, leaving out the noun's own shape $s^*$, which the context never names (for nouns without a fixed shape, a shape assigned to the noun at random is left out, so every score compares $s$ with the same number of shapes). It is in nats: 3.4 means the context's shape is about 30 times as likely as the average other shape. Each noun gets 36 items. Because log probabilities share one normaliser, a stored shape that only raises its own probability leaves the copy score unchanged; the score falls only if the stored shape pushes the copied one down. We therefore also report which shape the model prefers, $L(s^*) - L(s)$, and how strongly it has stored $s^*$: $L(s^*)$ minus the mean of the other shapes after an unrelated sentence (\textbf{stored preference}). In \textbf{other-noun items}, the query names another new noun with the same history; if copying is tied to the noun, these should score lower. We also score unseen nouns with a \textbf{typical embedding}: the mean of the trained kinds' embeddings plus a random offset of typical length.

\textbf{Predictions.} Table~\ref{tab:pred} gives what each account predicts. Only memory competition predicts that a stored shape lowers copying among spaced nouns. Useful context predicts that changing-shape nouns copy more in bursts than spaced. Bursts might also help for reasons unrelated to usefulness: the embedding is updated in fewer windows, and a test item, two consecutive sentences about one noun, resembles what burst nouns met in training and spaced nouns did not. Fixed-shape bursts share both features, but their shape can be stored, so the context is not needed. Memory competition also predicts that fixed-shape bursts copy less, but only by as much as a stored shape lowers copying among spaced nouns. Redrawn-shape bursts separate usefulness from recurrence: they recur and are updated like changing-shape bursts and store nothing, but their previous sentence is no guide.

\textbf{Analysis.} The seed is the unit: we average each condition over model sizes within a seed and test across seeds, with Holm correction within each registered family \citep{holm1979}, and count a prediction as supported only if its direction also holds in two of the three sizes. Each experiment was registered before any of its models was trained. These are all the experiments run with this design; earlier studies in the same language used other designs, and none of their data enter this paper. \section{Results}\label{sec:results}
Where Experiment~3 tested a comparison, we report its value; Table~\ref{tab:key} gives Experiment~2's beside it. Results not marked pre-registered are exploratory, including the control, the mechanism analyses, tied embeddings and shifting-kind corpora.

\subsection{The baseline: unseen nouns copy, familiar spaced nouns stop}\label{sec:rows}
Experiment~1 set the baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:M}, Figure~\ref{fig:carry}). In stable-kind corpora unseen nouns copied 4.53 nats [95\% CI 3.54,~5.52], but because of their embeddings: given a typical embedding, the same nouns scored $-$0.33 (Experiment~3: 6.88 and 0.79). Scaling an unseen noun's embedding to the trained kinds' average size, keeping its direction, lowered its score only from 4.53 to 4.00 (pre-registered and supported, almost entirely in the smallest models), and a random direction of that size kept copying (4.09), whereas the trained kinds' mean embedding removed it ($-$0.44). What matters most is what an embedding shares with the trained kinds' embeddings. With tied embeddings (ten 3.4M models; Appendix~\ref{app:O}), unseen nouns still copied (3.96 nats) and a typical embedding still removed it (0.02).

In stable-kind corpora, familiar nouns met one sentence at a time copied less the more exposures they had had: 3.6--4.5 nats at 10 exposures, under 1 nat at 100, and between $-$0.37 and 0.05 at 1,000 and 10,000, whatever their history. Changing-shape nouns, with nothing to store, copied about as little as fixed-shape nouns, which stored their shape well (stored preference 9.5 nats at 1,000 exposures); the registered prediction that fixed-shape nouns would lose copying faster than no-shape nouns was not supported. In random-order corpora no noun copied, so the copying is learned from the runs of sentences about a kind: within a run the previous sentence names the kind's shape, so copying pays until the shape is stored, and the trained kinds themselves end up not copying ($-$0.02). Over training, unseen nouns' copying grew (Figure~\ref{fig:a10formation}): nothing in an untrained embedding turns off a tendency the model builds for all nouns. What, then, keeps it on for a familiar noun?

\subsection{Copying stays high only where the context was needed}\label{sec:bursts}
\textbf{Bursts with changing shapes copy.} Changing-shape bursts, where a noun's previous sentence gives its current shape and memory cannot, scored 4.14 nats [3.60,~4.69] more than changing-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered; Figure~\ref{fig:a11}a), as much at 1,000 exposures as at 100. At 1,000 exposures they scored 4.71 nats, against at most 1.1 for every other familiar history. The contrast was sharpest in the smallest models: at 25.6M the other histories kept 0.4 to 1.7 nats, against 3.56 (Table~\ref{tab:O2}). The comparison between 100 and 1,000 exposures is between different nouns at the end of training. Over training, copying for these nouns grew: in Experiment~2, nouns with 1,000 exposures went from 2.6, 1.1 and 0.8 nats at the first saved weights to 4.9, 3.3 and 3.3 at the end (3.4M, 10.9M, 25.6M), while fixed-shape bursts stayed between 0.2 and 1.3 (Figure~\ref{fig:a10formation}).

\textbf{Not because bursts leave embeddings less trained.} Fixed-shape bursts have the same frame, presentation and number of updates, but a shape that memory can supply. At 1,000 exposures changing-shape bursts copied 3.68 nats [2.99,~4.37] more than fixed-shape bursts, at all three sizes (pre-registered): 6.36, 2.44 and 2.24 nats at 3.4M, 10.9M and 25.6M parameters, and 4.18 [2.95,~5.41] in ten models of 85.6M (pre-registered), which also had a longer warm-up and other hardware; as sizes differ in more than size, we do not read the pattern across them as a trend. With tied embeddings (3.4M) the pattern held but was weaker: changing-shape bursts copied 4.29 nats at 1,000 exposures, 1.90 [1.20,~2.60] more than fixed-shape bursts, while the other familiar histories kept 1.4 to 2.9 nats instead of losing their copying. Fixed-shape bursts copied only 0.57 nats [0.39,~0.74] more than fixed-shape spaced nouns at 1,000.

\textbf{Not because the noun recurs.} In the control, redrawn-shape bursts recur as changing-shape bursts do, but their previous sentence names the current shape only by chance (one time in ten). At 1,000 exposures they copied 0.49 nats, no more than changing-shape spaced nouns (0.07 [$-$0.01,~0.15] above them), and 3.33 [2.74,~3.92] less than changing-shape bursts in the same models, in all ten seeds and at every size (5.82, 2.04 and 2.14 nats; Figure~\ref{fig:a11}b, Table~\ref{tab:P1}). Half-kept bursts fell in between (1.20 nats), so copying rose with how often the previous sentence was right. Recurrence helped only early: at 100 exposures redrawn-shape bursts copied 0.96 nats [0.76,~1.15] more than spaced nouns, as no-shape bursts did in Experiment~2 (pre-registered there, both levels pooled; 0.20 [$-$0.10,~0.49] at 1,000). The control also replicated the main contrast: changing-shape bursts copied 3.01 [2.42,~3.59] more than fixed-shape bursts. It used Experiment~3's seeds with new corpora, so it is not fully independent of it.

\subsection{A stored shape wins at the output but barely suppresses copying}\label{sec:memory}
\textbf{At test.} Fixed- and changing-shape spaced nouns share frame, presentation and expected word class, and differ only in whether a shape can be stored. Fixed-shape spaced nouns copied 0.25 nats [0.15,~0.35] less (pre-registered), mostly at 100 exposures (0.40) rather than 1,000 (0.10): the stored shape pushed the copied one down a little, although we could not show the effect was below 0.3 nats. At the output, however, the stored shape won: fixed-shape spaced nouns preferred their own shape to the context's by 5.84 nats [5.57,~6.11] (pre-registered). Fixed- and changing-shape spaced nouns copied little in any case (under 2 nats). Fixed-shape spaced nouns also scored about 1 nat below no-shape spaced nouns (pre-registered in Experiment~2), but no-shape nouns expect a colour where the others expect a shape, so this difference reflects word class.

\textbf{In training.} Suppression at test cannot account for the loss of copying in fixed-shape bursts. At 1,000 exposures a stored shape lowered spaced nouns' copying by 0.10 nats, but the burst difference was 3.58 nats [2.94,~4.22] larger; even a proportional suppression (about a fifth) would predict a burst difference of about 0.9. No-shape bursts, which stored nothing, copied no more than fixed-shape bursts (1.05 against 1.03), as did redrawn-shape bursts in the control (0.49 against 0.82), and in tied models a stored shape did not lower spaced nouns' copying at all ($-$0.01). What a storable shape changed was what training kept: copying stayed high only where the context had been needed. Memory was not manipulated during training, so this is an inference from these comparisons.

\subsection{What is kept, and where}\label{sec:kept}
\textbf{A tendency, mostly not a link.} If changing-shape bursts bound a noun to its current shape, other-noun items should score lower. For changing-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures it did so by only 0.08 nats [0.02,~0.14], within $\pm$0.3 (pre-registered), and no other condition in mixed or fixed-only corpora exceeded 0.14: the model copied the latest shape after the query noun, whichever noun the context described, as training never required it to check. In changing-only corpora, however, changing-shape bursts showed a noun-specific part of 0.33 nats [0.18,~0.48].

\textbf{The query noun's history matters more.} With a context about a noun of one history and a query naming another, the query noun's history mattered more than the context noun's (1.72 against 0.47 nats for changing- versus fixed-shape bursts; difference 1.25 [0.94,~1.56], pre-registered).

\textbf{It depends on the other nouns.} In single-history corpora no noun of another history was present (Figure~\ref{fig:a11}c). There, changing-shape bursts copied 5.51 nats [4.24,~6.78] more than fixed-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures, at all three sizes (pre-registered), so the main difference does not need nouns of the other history. It was larger than in mixed corpora with the same seeds (4.27), because fixed-shape bursts lost all their copying when no noun of another history shared the corpus: 0.03 nats against 1.03, a difference of 1.00 [0.80,~1.20] (pre-registered). For changing-shape bursts this comparison was inconclusive. Unseen nouns copied less in both single-history designs than in mixed corpora (fixed-only 4.87, changing-only 6.16, mixed 7.35; five seeds each), so a noun's copying depends on which other nouns share the corpus, not only on its own history; why changing-only corpora also lowered unseen nouns' copying we cannot say.

\textbf{What in the embedding carries it.} All other weights are shared, so a difference between nouns in one model must enter through their input embeddings; the question is what about the embeddings carries it. Simple geometry does not. Within each model we regressed copy scores on each familiar noun's embedding's cosine with its initial value, its distance from the trained kinds' mean and its norm: at 1,000 exposures changing-shape bursts copied 3.67 nats [3.03,~4.32] more than these three measures predict (pre-registered), although their embeddings were only slightly closer to their initial values than those of fixed-shape bursts (cosine 0.85 against 0.78). This rules out these three summaries, not geometry in general. Three exploratory analyses go further (Appendix~\ref{app:O}). Part of the tendency lies along a direction shared by nouns: adding the difference between the mean embeddings of changing- and fixed-shape bursts, taken from other nouns, raised copying at 1,000 exposures by 1.51 nats [1.20,~1.82] for fixed-shape spaced nouns and 1.99 for unseen nouns with a typical embedding, while a random vector of the same length changed it by at most 0.02 (Table~\ref{tab:O4}). The direction mattered most at 3.4M (2.41 for fixed-shape spaced nouns, against 1.12 and 1.00 at 10.9M and 25.6M), and subtracting it from changing-shape bursts at 25.6M changed nothing (+0.02), so outside the smallest models it carries a minority of the effect. Patching hidden states \citep{zhang2024patching} showed that by the end of the second block the difference between histories had passed from the query noun's position to the position where the shape is predicted, as a readout through attention would require. There, attention in the first block to the context's shape word tracked copying within each model (mean Spearman $\rho$ = 0.80 [0.76,~0.85]); this is a correlation, and we did not identify the heads involved.

\textbf{It is narrow.} With one unrelated sentence between context and query, copying almost vanished (at most 0.44 nats). With the context in another frame (\emph{Look at the \textit{noun}. It is \textit{shape} and \textit{colour}.}) changing-shape bursts kept 35 to 40\% of it at 1,000 exposures. For colour, which varies freely within every kind, no noun copied.

\subsection{When the whole corpus rewards copying}\label{sec:beyond}
In Experiment~2's shifting-kind corpora, where the trained kinds' shapes can come only from context, nouns of every history in bursts kept copying (Figure~\ref{fig:a11}d): at 1,000 exposures fixed-shape bursts scored 3.57 nats, no-shape bursts 2.09 and changing-shape bursts 6.82, more than unseen nouns (5.02). Fixed-shape and no-shape bursts still copied less at 1,000 exposures than at 100 (5.35 and 3.79), while changing-shape bursts copied more (5.40 at 100). So whether a stored shape ends copying depends on the corpus: where the trained kinds themselves reward copying, it does not.


\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What decides copying.} In stable-kind corpora the model builds a tendency to copy the latest shape and turns it off, noun by noun, through the noun's embedding. It stays on for unseen nouns, whose embeddings are untrained, and for nouns whose own earlier sentences were the only source of their current shape; nouns met 1,000 times otherwise copied little. This held in new seeds, at 85.6M parameters and in corpora without nouns of the other history, most clearly in the smallest models and with untied embeddings, and it tracked usefulness rather than recurrence: bursts whose previous sentence was no guide lost copying like spaced nouns. Of the three accounts, memory competition at test explains little: a stored shape lowered copying by at most 0.4 nats in any experiment, about a tenth of the effect of a useful context, and acted mainly by winning in prediction. The representation account is right that the difference lives in the embedding, but wrong that familiarity alone ends copying: history mattered beyond three simple measures of the embedding's geometry. Useful context fits best, and the control rules out the alternative that recurrence alone keeps copying; memory acts through training: where a shape could be stored, the context was not needed, and copying was not kept.

\textbf{What this adds.} \citet{chan2022} showed that bursty items whose meaning changes favour in-context learning across a population of items. We find the same rule acting on single words within one model; per-word embeddings make this possible but do not require it. Three results go further. First, memory's effect fell on training, not on prediction: a stored shape barely suppressed the copied one at test, yet decided whether copying was kept. Second, what was kept was a tendency to copy, mostly not tied to the noun, and how much a noun kept depended on the other nouns in the corpus. Third, usefulness, not recurrence, kept copying in stable-kind corpora, in graded form: bursts kept more copying the more often their previous sentence was right. The lower copying of nouns met more often is a per-word counterpart of the transience of in-context learning \citep{singh2023}, and the role of the embedding fits the finding that resetting embeddings keeps in-context use alive \citep{anand2025}.

\textbf{Implications for evaluation.} Probes with invented words are common. In small models trained from scratch, such a probe can measure the untrained embedding more than anything learned about context, so effects should be checked against a typical embedding, itself an intervention (Limitations). A registered test in Pythia \citep{biderman2023} found no consistent effect of replacing real nouns' embeddings (Appendix~\ref{app:N}, Figure~\ref{fig:pythia}), so our evidence for this caution comes from small models. More generally, whether a model copies a word's features from context depends on that word's own training history, so tests of in-context use should report how the probe words occurred in training. Natural text resembles changing-shape bursts in one respect: in WikiText-103 (introduced by \citealp{merity2017}), the 44 nouns of the pretrained test recur in bursts, and a colour named twice for a noun within one article is usually the same, whereas two mentions from anywhere agree less often (exploratory; Appendix~\ref{app:O}).

\textbf{Analogies with children.} Two findings echo children's word learning, as analogies only. Changing-shape bursts resemble the boxes of \citet{dewar2010}, uniform within and varying between; after one draw from a new box, 9-month-olds expected the next to match, an overhypothesis in the sense of \citet{kemp2007}. And spaced nouns first stored their shape better, while bursts kept the context in use; children retain words better from spaced exposures \citep{childers2002,vlach2008} but learn labels repeated in successive sentences \citep{schwab2016}.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three features and five sentence frames; the models are small (at most 85.6M parameters) and trained with one recipe. Experiments~2 and~3 tested 100 and 1,000 exposures; comparisons across exposures are between different nouns, and over training exposure is confounded with training time. The learned copying almost vanished beyond one sentence, transferred only partly to another frame, and did not apply to colour. No-shape comparisons are confounded with expected word class, and bursts weakened stored shapes at 100 exposures. The test of memory competition rests on spaced nouns, which copied little. Single-history corpora used five seeds. With tied embeddings (one size, added after the results), familiar nouns kept more copying, so the loss of copying is clearest with untied embeddings. A typical embedding suppresses copying even in shifting-kind corpora, where the trained kinds copy strongly, so it may be atypical in its own way. The effect shrank from 3.4M to 25.6M parameters, but sizes also differ in steps, batch, learning rate and amount of text, and the larger 85.6M effect came with a longer warm-up and other hardware. The control was added after review and was not pre-registered (its design and predictions were fixed before training); it reused Experiment~3's seeds and tested three repeat rates. The mechanism analyses were added after the results. The mechanism is located only coarsely; the heads involved were not identified, and a registered search for induction-like heads in Experiment~1 found none (Figure~\ref{fig:heads}). Planned power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs and colour only, and the WikiText-103 counts include many names (\emph{Black Sea}). Earlier experiments in this language, not reported here, were registered in stages (Appendix~\ref{app:reg}).
