\title{Familiar Nouns Stop Taking Features from Context:\\A Registered Study in Small Transformers}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Small transformers trained on text in which nearby sentences describe the same kind learn to carry a feature from one mention of a noun to the next. Probes of this use nonce nouns, but a nonce noun is both unfamiliar and without stored knowledge. We separated the two in a registered experiment. In 90 models (3.4M to 25.6M parameters, ten seeds) trained on an artificial language, 40 nonce nouns appeared 0 to 10,000 times: with one fixed shape, with a new shape every time, or never with any shape. Carry of a shape given in the context was strong for unseen nouns and had gone by 100 exposures for every kind of noun. Nouns never seen with a shape lost it as fully as nouns with a fixed shape and ended within 0.33 nats of unseen nouns given an embedding typical of trained nouns, equivalent within a registered 1-nat bound. Stored knowledge of the fixed shape grew to about 10 nats but did not speed the loss. When training made the context useful for familiar nouns, they kept some carry, and nouns with a stored or unpredictable shape kept less (exploratory). Ablating early heads chosen by an induction-head signature did not selectively remove carry. Earlier experiments with the same language show that effects measured with unseen tokens largely reflect how those tokens are represented.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A language model can take a word's properties from the text around it or from what it has stored about the word. Which it does depends on its training data. Classes that occur in bursts or rarely favour learning in context, and common classes favour learning stored in the weights \citep{chan2022,chan2025}; in pretrained models, heads that copy from the context compete with recall from memory \citep{yu2023,jin2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask about a single word: as a model sees a noun more often, when does it stop taking the noun's features from the context, and why?

Two things change as a model sees a word. Its representation becomes like those of other words it knows, and it may store what the word's referents are like. In natural data the two go together, and probes inherit the confound: a nonce word tests generalisation to a new item \citep{thrush2020}, but it is new in representation as well as in knowledge, and conclusions drawn with nonce words can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}. We met this confound in our own earlier experiments (Section~\ref{sec:background}). Small transformers trained on grouped text carried a shape from one mention of an unseen noun to the next, but most of that carry vanished when the same noun was given an embedding typical of trained nouns.

We therefore separated familiarity from stored knowledge by design. In every model, nonce nouns appeared a set number of times (0, 10, 100, 1,000 or 10,000) and, independently, either always with one shape, with a new shape every time, or never with any shape. All hypotheses, tests and the analysis were registered before any corpus was built. We find:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Familiarity switched carry off.} Strong for unseen nouns, carry had gone by 100 exposures for every kind of noun, including nouns never seen with a shape.
\item \textbf{Stored knowledge did not add to it.} A fixed shape was learned strongly but carry fell no faster for it, and familiar nouns without a shape ended where an unseen noun with a typical embedding sits.
\item \textbf{When the context was useful, history mattered.} In training where a familiar noun's shape could only come from the context, familiar nouns kept some carry, and less when their own history gave a stored or an unpredictable shape (exploratory).
\item \textbf{We did not find the heads.} Early heads chosen by an induction-head signature did not selectively remove carry, and no head showed prefix matching.
\end{enumerate}
Our claims are about transformers trained on an artificial language, not about children or large pretrained models.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning stored in the weights \citep{chan2022}. It favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023,anand2025}, and its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}. We manipulate familiarity and stored knowledge for single words within one model.

\textbf{Context against memory.} In pretrained models, attention heads that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts, and removing a few heads can shift the balance \citep{yu2023,jin2024}. Frequency in such work is observed rather than manipulated.

\textbf{Nonce words and exposure.} Nonce words are the usual test of generalisation to a new item \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and controlling how often an item occurs in training shows how frequency shapes what transformers learn \citep{wei2021}. We control exposure exactly, down to the window in which each occurrence is read.

\textbf{Arrangement and repeated data.} Packing related documents together changes how models use context \citep{shi2024,zhao2024}, and repeated passes over fixed data change what is learned \citep{murty2023,charton2024}. Our earlier experiments vary only arrangement (Section~\ref{sec:background}).

\section{Setting and earlier experiments}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by a nonce noun, with 14 exemplars per kind. Each exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each a nonce word from a list of ten, and is described in one of five frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (Appendix~\ref{app:I}). In \textbf{Regular} corpora each kind keeps one shape. In \textbf{Reassigned} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass through the corpus and keeps it within the pass, so only the context can tell a familiar noun's current shape. In \textbf{random} order every pass is shuffled; in \textbf{grouped} order sentences about a kind come in runs of four (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). Each pass is arranged afresh and read in non-overlapping 128-token windows, so no text is read twice. Models are decoder-only transformers of 3.4M, 11.0M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers; Appendix~\ref{app:C}). Every artificial word is one token.

\textbf{Earlier results.} In registered experiments with 120 models (five seeds), we gave a model one sentence about an object of a new kind and measured how much more it expected the next mention of that noun to keep the shape than the colour (Appendices~\ref{app:A} and~\ref{app:E}). With nouns never seen in training, grouping raised this expectation over random order by 11.08 nats [9.57,~12.59], only when kinds had a fixed feature, and when Reassigned training made the context necessary, trained nouns carried the context's shape only after the same noun (Table~\ref{tab:contrasts}; Appendix~\ref{app:L}). The direction of these effects held when the test nouns were given an embedding typical of trained nouns, and for trained nouns, but their size did not (exploratory; Appendix~\ref{app:L}). In grouped Regular models the preference to repeat the context's shape was 6.88 nats for unseen nouns and \textminus{}0.72 for the same nouns with a typical embedding, and it fell steadily as we blended the two embeddings ($-7.8$ nats per unit blend). The unseen nouns' input embeddings had never been trained, because a tie between input and output embeddings was lost on the training hardware. So the earlier probes measured arrangement and the test noun's representation together. Models trained instead on one fixed file, read 160 to 290 times, memorised it, and their effects differed in size (Appendix~\ref{app:C}).

\section{The familiarity study}\label{sec:design}
\textbf{Nouns.} We added 40 nonce nouns to three base corpora (Regular grouped, Regular random and Reassigned grouped). In every model they fill 13 cells: three kinds of history (Table~\ref{tab:design}), each at 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, three nouns per cell, plus four unseen nouns. For comparison, a trained kind appears in about 60,000 sentences. Nouns rotate over cells across seeds. A sixth frame, \emph{The \textit{noun} is \textit{colour} and \textit{texture}}, names no shape; it is the only frame of shape-absent nouns and is also used by the trained kinds once in fifteen sentences, so it is not new.

\textbf{Exact exposure.} Each corpus holds exactly as many 128-token windows as training reads, and a fixed permutation says when each is read. Each familiarity sentence lies wholly inside one window, at most one per window and only between runs, and a noun's exposures are evenly spaced from 5\% to 95\% of training. A checker re-read every corpus and verified each exposure, window and frame; the corpora matched checksums recorded before training. We trained 90 models: three sizes, ten seeds, three base corpora, with input and output embeddings untied in code.

\textbf{Measures.} Each item gives one context sentence, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a $s$ \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}, then \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the log probability $L$ of each of the ten shapes at the next position. \textbf{Carry} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over shapes other than $s$ and $s^*$; \textbf{stored preference} is $L(s^*)$ minus the same mean; \textbf{knowledge} is $L(s^*)$ minus the mean over the other nine shapes after an unrelated sentence. The context shape is never $s^*$, and each of the other nine shapes appears four times per noun. Unseen nouns are also scored with an embedding typical of trained nouns, as in the earlier study.

\textbf{Hypotheses and analysis.} In grouped Regular models we predicted that carry would fall faster with exposure for fixed-shape than for shape-absent nouns (H30); tested whether shape-absent nouns at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures differ in carry from unseen nouns with a typical embedding, with an equivalence test against $\pm$1 nat (H31); and predicted that stored preference rises with exposure (H32). A manipulation check (M1) required knowledge of at least 0.2 nats at 1,000 exposures. Head hypotheses (H33 to H35) are in Section~\ref{sec:heads}. The seed is the unit of replication: we average cell means over the three sizes within each seed and test over ten seeds, with \citet{holm1979} correction across H30 to H35. A hypothesis is supported only if its direction also holds at two of the three sizes. A one-seed pilot, never used in a test, set the number of nouns per cell; its expected power for a 1-nat difference was 0.57, below the planned 0.8 (Appendix~\ref{app:M}).

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering

\caption{Carry of the context's shape by exposure and noun history (nats; means over ten seeds of size-averaged cell means, 95\% intervals). Diamond: unseen nouns. Dashed line: unseen nouns with an embedding typical of trained nouns.}
\label{fig:carry}

\textbf{The manipulation worked.} Fixed-shape nouns learned their shape: knowledge at 1,000 exposures was 9.53, 8.61 and 8.26 nats in Regular grouped, Regular random and Reassigned grouped models, rising with exposure in all three (M1). In grouped Regular models, nouns with an uninformative or no shape learned none (within 0.2 nats; Table~\ref{tab:M3}).

\textbf{Familiarity switched carry off.} In grouped Regular models, carry was 4.53 nats [3.54,~5.52] for unseen nouns and had nearly gone by 100 exposures for every history (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left; Table~\ref{tab:M2}). At 10 exposures it was 3.6 to 4.5 nats; at 100, 0.16 to 0.86; from 1,000 on, within 0.4 nats of zero. Grouping, not familiarity, created the carry: in random order carry was near zero for every noun, and grouped models carried more than random ones for unseen nouns (4.68 [3.71,~5.65]; R1, replicating the earlier study).

\textbf{Stored knowledge did not add to it.} Carry did not fall faster for fixed-shape nouns than for shape-absent ones. The slope difference was 0.16 nats per decade [0.01,~0.30], in the opposite direction to H30 at every size, so H30 was not supported. Familiar shape-absent nouns, which never saw a shape, carried 0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52] more than unseen nouns with a typical embedding (H31: Holm $p=.013$, positive at all three sizes), and the two were equivalent within the registered 1-nat bound (TOST $p<.001$). Stored preference rose by 2.97 nats per decade of exposure [2.79,~3.16] for fixed-shape nouns (H32, Holm $p<.001$), reaching 10.71 nats at 10,000 exposures; it changed nothing about carry. In these models, then, a noun stopped taking its shape from the context once it was familiar, whatever it had learned about that shape, and a familiar noun behaved almost exactly like an unseen noun given a typical embedding.

\textbf{When the context was useful, history mattered (exploratory).} In Reassigned training a trained kind's current shape can come only from the context, and trained kinds carried it strongly (9.98 nats). Here familiarity reduced carry but did not switch it off (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, middle). Shape-absent nouns kept 2.04 and 1.86 nats at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, again close to unseen nouns with a typical embedding (difference 0.38 [\textminus{}0.44,~1.19]). Nouns whose history said something about shape kept less: fixed-shape nouns carried 1.31 nats [0.89,~1.72] less than shape-absent nouns, and uninformative nouns, whose shape had changed in every sentence, 0.42 [0.36,~0.47] less again (1,000 and 10,000 exposures pooled). So familiarity set a baseline equal to that of a typical embedding, and a noun's own history moved carry below it only when the baseline was above zero. In grouped Regular models the baseline was already near zero, which leaves H30 little room; we return to this in the limitations.

\subsection{Heads}\label{sec:heads}
We registered a search for heads that carry the shape. In each model, before any familiarity item was scored, we chose the $k$ heads ($k$ = 2, 4 or 6 by size) with the highest product of a prefix-matching score and a copying score on repeated random sequences of the language's words, the signature of induction heads \citep{olsson2022}, and replaced their output by its mean over ordinary sentences at the position that predicts the shape. H33 predicted that this would remove more than half of the carry of unseen nouns in grouped Regular models, and more than ablating random heads of the same layers; H34 that it would reduce trained kinds' carry in Reassigned models; and H35, given H33, that the heads' attention to the context's shape would fall faster with exposure for fixed-shape nouns.

H33 was not supported. Every candidate was in the first layer, and no head in any model showed prefix matching above the level of uniform attention (highest score 0.043 against about 0.027; Table~\ref{tab:M4}), so these are at most copying heads, not induction heads. Ablating them removed 0.22 [0.07,~0.36] of unseen carry, against the registered 0.5, and no more than random heads did (difference 0.64 [\textminus{}0.43,~1.71]). The result varied with size: ablation removed all unseen carry in the 3.4M models (8.10 to \textminus{}0.42 nats, random heads 5.26) but raised it in the 11.0M models (2.98 to 5.80). H34 was supported: in Reassigned models ablation cut trained kinds' carry by 7.41 nats [6.02,~8.80]. But random heads cut it as much (to 2.5 to 3.4 nats from about 10), so the effect is not specific to the candidates. H35 was not tested. The candidates' attention to the context's shape fell with familiarity in the way carry did (Table~\ref{tab:M5}; exploratory): in grouped Regular models from 0.155 for unseen nouns to about 0.07 from 1,000 exposures for every history, and in Reassigned models it stayed highest for shape-absent nouns. First-layer attention to the shape word therefore tracks carry (Figure~\ref{fig:heads}), but our registered ablations do not show that any specific heads produce it.

\section{Discussion}
In these models a noun stopped taking its shape from the context once it was familiar, and what it had stored about that shape did not decide when. A familiar noun that never appeared with a shape behaved like an unseen noun given an ordinary-looking embedding. This locates the large carry measured with unseen nouns in the earlier study: it was carry for a token unlike any trained noun, and it ends when the token's representation becomes ordinary, whether that comes from training on the noun or from giving it a typical embedding. It refines the trade-off between in-context and in-weights learning \citep{chan2022,chan2025}: for single words in our language, what switched in-context use off was familiarity, not a stored answer competing with the context.

Stored knowledge mattered only when familiarity left room. In Reassigned training, where context was useful for familiar nouns, a familiar noun kept a moderate carry, and its own history lowered it: a stored shape competed with the context, and a shape that had changed in every sentence taught the model not to rely on the context for that noun. That second result is a noun-specific lesson about how a feature varies, a small version of what hierarchical models call an overhypothesis \citep{kemp2007}; we note it as exploratory.

For work with nonce words, the lesson is that an unseen token is not a neutral stand-in for a new word. Its untrained or unusual representation can carry much of an effect, and effects should be checked with representations that look familiar. For mechanistic work, an induction-head signature did not find the components that carry a kind's feature in our models; the carry appears to depend on first-layer attention to feature words, but establishing that would need a different, causal search.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three dimensions and six frames; the models are small (3.4M to 25.6M parameters) and trained with one recipe. We do not know whether the familiarity effect holds in pretrained models. In grouped Regular models carry of familiar nouns was near zero from 100 exposures, so H30 had little room to show an effect of stored knowledge; the evidence that knowledge matters when there is room comes from exploratory Reassigned results. Exposure levels were spaced by factors of ten, and carry fell between 10 and 100 exposures, which we did not sample. Power for a 1-nat difference was below plan (0.57). Unseen nouns in the earlier study had untrained embeddings because an embedding tie was lost; in the familiarity study embeddings were untied by design. The head search used one registered signature and mean ablation; other methods might find components we missed. The earlier experiments were registered in stages, some after related results were known (Appendix~\ref{app:A}); the familiarity study was registered before any corpus was built, with details fixed in a dated addendum before data and a pilot that set only the number of nouns per cell.
