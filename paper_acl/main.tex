\title{Same Sentences, Different Order: What the Arrangement of\\Training Text Teaches Small Transformers}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Training pipelines arrange text when they pack documents into windows, order them or generate synthetic data, but arrangement is rarely tested with content held fixed. We trained small transformers on artificial languages in which the same sentences came in different orders, arranging every pass afresh so that no training window was seen twice. After one description of an object of a new kind, models trained on random order expected the next object of that kind to have a different shape (shape-over-colour index \textminus{}1.56 nats); trained on the same sentences grouped by kind, they expected the same shape (9.52). The gain needed a feature fixed within kinds and appeared at moderate clustering, and runs that described different objects of a kind taught it more selectively than runs that described one object again. The models tied the expectation to the noun only when the data required it. Models trained for many passes over one fixed file memorised it, and the effects of arrangement then changed in size and, in random order, in sign. Object labels are more clustered in speech to young children than in written text, and pretrained Pythia models show only a partial analogue. What is placed near what in training data, not only what is included, shapes what a model generalises.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Every training pipeline arranges its text. It packs documents into windows, orders them and, more and more, generates synthetic text whose layout someone chooses \citep{shi2024,zhao2024}. Work on training data mostly asks about content: which sources, how much, and how close to a child's input \citep{warstadt2023,feng2024}. Arrangement is rarely tested on its own, with content held fixed, although natural text differs in it. A word that has appeared once in a document is far more likely to appear again soon than its frequency predicts \citep{church2000}, and parents' talk to toddlers returns to the same objects in clusters \citep{slone2023}. In transformers, such burstiness decides whether in-context learning emerges \citep{chan2022}. We ask a narrower question: with the same sentences, what does arrangement change in the expectations a model stores in its weights, and which properties of an arrangement matter?

We need a regularity that holds across items, so that arrangement can make it easier or harder to see. We borrow one from word learning. A child who hears ``this is a dax'' about a new object will call other objects ``dax'' if they share its shape, even when they differ in texture \citep{landau1988}; the child has learned that things with the same name tend to share a shape \citep{kemp2007}. We give a model one description of an object of a new kind, such as \emph{A snerg is a krannish pulek skorli thing}, where the three adjectives name a shape, a colour and a texture, and ask how much more it expects a second snerg to keep the shape than to keep the colour. We use this only as a test of what transformers learn from their data. Our claims are about transformers, not children.

A positive score can come from two expectations. One is tied to the noun: objects with the same name share a shape. The other is not: whatever object comes next keeps the last one's shape. We separate them by changing the noun at test.

We trained 510 small transformers on artificial languages whose content and order we control, measured the same arrangement statistic in natural corpora, and tested pretrained models (we call these Studies 1, 2 and 3, the names used in our registrations). We report four findings:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item With content fixed, arrangement decided which feature the models expected to persist. Random order taught them to expect a shape to change; grouping by kind taught them to expect it to stay. The gain needed a feature fixed within kinds and appeared at moderate clustering.
\item Nearby descriptions of \emph{different} objects of a kind taught the regularity selectively; repeated descriptions of \emph{one} object also taught the models to expect colour to repeat, and so taught less selectively.
\item The models tied the expectation to the noun only when the noun was needed: barely when each kind kept one shape, and, for nouns seen in training, about as strongly as the text warranted when a kind's shape changed between stretches of text.
\item Models trained for many passes over one short fixed file memorised it, and the measured effects of arrangement changed in size and, in random order, in sign. Text arranged afresh for every pass avoids this.
\end{enumerate}
For anyone building training data, the results point to a specific aim for regularities that hold across items: place different instances of a kind near each other, rather than repeated descriptions of one instance. Every confirmatory test was registered before the models concerned were trained or scored; some registrations were written after earlier results had been seen, and Appendix~\ref{app:A} gives the dated record of what was known at each.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{Arrangement and order.} Packing related documents into one window improves how pretrained models use their context \citep{shi2024,zhao2024}. Curricula that order examples by difficulty have given mixed results \citep{bengio2009,rohde1999,diehlmartinez2023}. At child scale, the local order of child-directed speech affected what models learned, while its global order by age did not \citep{feng2024}, and generated variation sets had mixed effects \citep{haga2024}. We hold content fixed and vary only local arrangement.

\textbf{Burstiness and in-context learning.} Words are bursty \citep{church1995,church2000}, and cache models exploited this \citep{kuhn1990}. \citet{chan2022} found that in-context learning emerged when items of a class appeared in bursts, especially when item meanings were dynamic rather than fixed; \citet{reddy2024} traced it to induction heads, and \citet{singh2023} showed it can fade as learning in the weights takes over. Those studies ask what a model does with examples in its context. We ask what arrangement sets up in the weights before the context offers any evidence.

\textbf{Artificial languages and repeated data.} Artificial languages let a model's preferences be traced to its input \citep{mccoy2020,white2021,kallini2024}. Such studies typically reuse one generated corpus over many passes, from about two to a few dozen in these examples \citep{mccoy2020,kallini2024,murty2023}. The number of passes matters: training long on fixed data changed hierarchical generalisation \citep{murty2023}, at a fixed budget, small sets of repeated examples beat larger sets of single-use examples on synthetic maths tasks \citep{charton2024}, and at scale up to about four epochs of repeated data changed loss negligibly \citep{muennighoff2023}. The BabyLM Challenge capped training at ten epochs in 2025, judging tens or hundreds of repeated exposures developmentally implausible \citep{charpentier2025}. We add a case where the number of passes changed the measured effect of arrangement.

\textbf{Shape bias in networks.} Networks trained on images or artificial objects can show a shape bias \citep{ritter2017,feinman2018}, and a hierarchical Bayesian model learns it from how kinds vary \citep{kemp2007}. We ask whether a next-word learner acquires it from descriptions in text, and what arrangement it needs.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Corpora}
Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by a novel noun (\emph{wug}, \emph{dax}, \ldots), with 14 exemplars per kind. Each exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each a novel word from a list of ten, and is described by one sentence in one of five frames (Figure~\ref{fig:1}; Appendix~\ref{app:I}). Shape is named in all five frames, colour in three and texture in one; the frames are the same in every corpus we compare, so every conclusion rests on comparisons between corpora. The 448 sentences of a corpus, each used once, make up one pass.

\textbf{Content.} In \textbf{Regular} corpora each kind keeps one shape while colour and texture vary. In \textbf{No stable feature} corpora each exemplar's shape is drawn at random, so nothing is fixed within a kind. In \textbf{Colour stable} corpora each kind keeps one colour instead. \textbf{Objects} corpora give each kind 13 objects and describe each object four times. In \textbf{Reassigned} corpora each kind gets a new shape in every pass and keeps it for that pass, so the next sentence about a kind still repeats its shape, but the noun no longer says which shape it will be.

\textbf{Arrangement.} In \textbf{random} order every pass is shuffled. In \textbf{grouped} order the sentences about each kind are cut into runs of four, and the runs are shuffled across kinds; every sentence occurs as often as in random order. \textbf{Graded} orders keep each run with a set probability, giving intermediate clustering. In Objects corpora a \textbf{same-object run} is the four descriptions of one object and a \textbf{different-object run} is four objects of one kind.

\textbf{Clustering.} Take consecutive mentions of object nouns no more than 100 words apart. Let $P$ be the share of these pairs that name the same kind, and $P_0$ the same share after the sentences of each document are shuffled. Clustering is $B = P/P_0$; $B = 1$ means no clustering beyond what the content predicts. Random order gives $B = 0.9$ and grouped order 23. Graded orders were set to match natural corpora (Section~\ref{sec:natural}).

\textbf{Fresh and fixed text.} Our main models were trained on fresh text: 7,500 passes of a corpus (2,100 for Objects), each arranged with its own random draw, 34 to 35 million tokens in all, read in non-overlapping 128-token windows so that no window was seen twice. A second set was trained in the common way, on one fixed file of 11,648 sentences (about 120,000 tokens), with windows at random positions, so that each position was seen 160 to 290 times. Some conditions (Colour stable, the finer graded orders and several controls) exist only as fixed files (Table~\ref{tab:1} in Appendix~\ref{app:I}).

\subsection{Models and test}\label{sec:test}
We trained decoder-only transformers of 3.4, 11.0 and 25.6 million parameters (4 to 8 layers) for 5,000 to 10,000 steps, with five seeds per condition at each size; a seed sets the corpus, the weights and the batch order (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). Every artificial word is one token.

Each test item gives the model one sentence describing an exemplar of a new kind, then two continuations that differ in one word: one repeats the probed feature and one does not (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). $\Delta$ is the difference in summed log probability between them, the model's preference for repeating that feature, in nats. Trained models generally prefer words they have just seen, so we use the difference between features:
\begin{equation*}
\text{index} = \Delta_{\text{shape}} - \Delta_{\text{colour}},
\end{equation*}
which cancels copying that affects both equally. With one exemplar, nothing in the context shows which feature is fixed, so differences between conditions come from training. Each item is also given with a different noun in the continuations; the \textbf{noun-specific index} is the index after the same noun minus the index after a different noun. A noun-tied expectation scores above zero; a noun-blind one scores zero. Test nouns are either novel or taken from training; novel nouns have untrained input embeddings (Appendix~\ref{app:F}). Untrained models give an index of 0.02 nats.

\textbf{Statistics.} Models of three sizes with the same seed share a corpus, so the five seeds are the units of replication. We average sizes within each seed and compare conditions by paired $t$ tests over the five seed means; differences and 95\% intervals in the text are these seed-level estimates. Registered contrasts were one-sided with \citet{holm1979} correction within each registered family. For fresh-text models the registered test was the seed-level test; for fixed-file models it was a linear mixed model or a Welch test over models, with seed-level results beside them in Appendix~\ref{app:E}. Results given with a Holm $p$, or called registered, were registered; all others are exploratory. Every contrast reported as reliable had the same direction at all three model sizes (Appendix~\ref{app:E}). Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the registered tests; Appendix~\ref{app:A} lists all 32.

\subsection{Arrangement decides which feature persists}\label{sec:persist}
In random order, Regular models came to expect the shape to \emph{change}: their index was \textminus{}1.56 nats [\textminus{}2.45,~\textminus{}0.67], below zero in all 15 models, against our prediction (Table~\ref{tab:2}). The same sentences in grouped order gave 9.52, higher in all five seeds and at every model size (difference 11.08 [9.57,~12.59], Holm $p<.001$). Clustering at $B = 3.5$, close to that of object labels in speech to children, already gave 1.28 (difference from random 2.84 [1.41,~4.27], Holm $p=.005$).

The gain needed a fixed feature. With nothing fixed within kinds, grouped models expected the shape to change, at \textminus{}3.98 (difference from grouped Regular 13.50 [11.48,~15.52], Holm $p<.001$). With colour fixed instead (fixed-file models only), the preference reversed: grouped Colour stable models favoured colour, at \textminus{}3.20, below fixed-file No stable feature models at \textminus{}0.44 (difference 2.76 [1.99,~3.52], Holm $p<.001$), expecting colour to repeat ($\Delta$ 2.39) and shape not to (\textminus{}0.81). Across the six orders of fixed-file Regular corpora, the index rose with $\log B$ at every step (slope 1.63 [1.46,~1.80] nats per log unit, Holm $p<.001$; Figure~\ref{fig:2}).

The corpora show what the models had to go on. In grouped order the next sentence is about the same kind 72\% of the time, and then always repeats the shape; in random order it is about the same kind 3\% of the time. A learner that predicted each feature from the previous sentence alone, ignoring the noun, gives a reference index that the models tracked across conditions (Pearson $r = .88$ over the eight fresh-text conditions, .95 over 12 fixed-file conditions; exploratory and descriptive, since conditions share seeds, and driven mainly by the contrast between grouped and ungrouped orders; Appendix~\ref{app:E}). The reference is near zero for random order and for No stable feature corpora, so it does not explain their negative indices, for which we have no tested account. The models also learned how long runs last. With two or four sentences about the new kind in the context, the index of grouped Regular models fell from 9.52 to 7.88 and 4.24: runs are four sentences long, so after four sentences about a kind the next is about the same kind only 3\% of the time.

\subsection{Different objects, not the same object again}
At matched clustering, runs of different objects gave a larger index than runs describing one object four times: 7.60 against 5.87 (difference 1.72 [0.54,~2.90], Holm $p=.008$; higher in all five seeds). Fixed-file models agreed (5.65 against 2.99). The components show why. After same-object runs, models expected colour to repeat more ($\Delta$ colour 0.99 against 0.46), because in those runs every feature does repeat; their index stayed positive because they repeated shape, named in more frames, more than the corpus warrants. Part of this may be copying. Repetition teaches selectively which feature persists when it brings different objects of a kind; re-describing one object teaches that colour persists too, and so teaches less selectively.

\subsection{The noun carries it only when needed}\label{sec:noun}
With novel test nouns, the index was about the same after the same noun and after a different noun in every corpus with a fixed shape (grouped Regular: noun-specific index 0.12 [0.04,~0.21] against an index of 9.52). This does not show that the expectation ignores the noun, because novel nouns have untrained embeddings; we therefore also used nouns from training, each given a shape it never has in training. Grouped Regular models then gave a noun-specific index of 1.04 [0.76,~1.31] (a registered secondary analysis), mostly from colour ($\Delta$ shape 0.06 after the same noun against \textminus{}0.08 after a different one). In these corpora a kind keeps one shape for the whole corpus, so the noun alone predicts it, and a model need not compare a sentence with the one before.

Reassigned corpora remove that route: a kind's shape changes from pass to pass, so only the previous sentence about the kind shows which shape comes next. Trained on them in grouped order, the models tied shape repetition to the noun. With nouns from training, the noun-specific index was 11.24 [10.44,~12.03] (Holm $p<.001$), close to the 11.4 the corpus warrants and about ten times the value with a fixed shape; the models expected shape to repeat after the same noun ($\Delta$ shape 10.01) and not after a different one (0.08). With novel nouns the effect was small but reliable (0.37 [0.24,~0.49], Holm $p=.001$), and larger than in grouped Regular models (0.25 [0.10,~0.39], Holm $p=.005$), though not in the largest models. The noun-blind index was unchanged by reassignment (9.80 against 9.52), and in random order reassignment taught neither a preference for shape (0.21) nor a dependence on the noun.

\subsection{Fixed files memorise and change the result}
Fixed-file models memorised their files. Their loss was 0.08 to 0.11 nats per token on their own file but 0.66 to 1.26 on a new arrangement of the same sentences; fresh-text models scored 0.28 to 0.55 on both (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). The arrangement effects changed with it. The grouping effect was about half the size (difference 6.02 [5.24,~6.79] against 11.08), and in random order the index was positive (0.47) rather than negative (\textminus{}1.56), though not reliably above zero (Holm $p=.061$). Early in training, while their loss was near that of fresh-text models, fixed-file random-order models also gave \textminus{}0.4 to \textminus{}1.5 nats; their index rose above zero only late, as loss fell to the memorised level (exploratory). Every contrast measured in both regimes had the same direction, but not the same size, and random order alone would have suggested a weak preference for shape rather than a clear expectation that shape changes. This adds to evidence that passes over fixed data change what a model generalises \citep{murty2023,charton2024}: here they changed the measured effect of arrangement itself.

\textbf{Natural corpora (Study 2).} With the same statistic, we measured how clustered mentions of 70 object nouns are in speech recorded around children under three (CHILDES; \citealp{macwhinney2000}), adult conversation (CABank) and written text (the Pile; \citealp{gao2020}). Labels were clustered in all three, most in speech to children ($B$ = 3.57 [3.13,~4.30]; above written text, Holm $p<.001$; Table~\ref{tab:natural}). But a repeated label need not bring a new object. In 500 hand-coded pairs, nearly half of the repetitions in speech to children returned to the same object, and a quarter brought a different one. Discounted accordingly, effective clustering of different objects was 1.95, 1.66 and 1.22, which places all three corpora between random order and $B = 3.6$ on our scale, where the fixed-file index rose only from 0.47 to 1.43 nats (Figure~\ref{fig:2}). Speech to children differed more in density: about 2.8 nearby different-object repetitions per 1,000 words, against 0.1 to 0.2 elsewhere (exploratory; partly a result of our choice of nouns). Appendix~\ref{app:J} gives the method and checks.

\textbf{Pretrained models (Study 3).} On English versions of the items, the shape-over-colour index of Pythia models \citep{biderman2023} rose with size (Spearman $\rho = .92$, Holm $p=.003$); on items whose two words have the same number of tokens (exploratory), every model from 1B parameters was above zero (from 1.00 nats at 1B to 1.62 at 12B). It did not rise steadily over training: at every size it first fell to about \textminus{}2 nats, as in our random-order models, and only larger models recovered. No Pythia model, nor Qwen2.5 72B or Llama 3.1 70B, preferred shape over texture, and evidence that they tied the expectation to the noun was weak and depended on wording (Appendix~\ref{app:K}). We did not arrange their data, so this is a comparison, not a test.

\section{Discussion}
Small transformers learned what the next sentence usually does. In grouped order the next sentence is often about another member of the same kind, its fixed feature can be predicted from the sentence before, and the models learned to carry that feature forward; in random order they learned to expect it to change. Which feature was carried followed the content, and the expectation was more selective when nearby sentences brought different objects than when they re-described one object. We identify the training conditions that produce the behaviour, not its mechanism; carrying a word forward is the kind of behaviour attributed to induction heads \citep{olsson2022}, but we did not examine attention.

Whether the models tied the expectation to the noun depended on whether the data required it. When the noun alone predicted a kind's shape, they barely did; when a kind's shape changed between passes, they did so, for nouns seen in training, almost exactly as much as the text warranted; with untrained test nouns the effect was small. This parallels the finding that in-context learning is stronger when item meanings are dynamic rather than fixed \citep{chan2022}, here for the features of a kind, with content otherwise held fixed.

People show related patterns: blocked study helps learners find what members of a category share \citep{carvalho2014}, and varied exemplars of each category taught toddlers more than near-identical ones \citep{perry2010}. We note the parallel without taking our results as evidence about how children learn.

Two practical points follow. First, arrangement is a lever on what a model generalises, separate from content: to teach a regularity that holds across items, place different instances of a kind near each other rather than repeated descriptions of one instance, and make an expectation depend on the kind if it should. Second, small-scale experiments on data should report how many passes they make over fixed text, and can avoid memorisation by arranging each pass afresh at no cost in content.

\section*{Limitations}
The artificial language has 32 kinds, three dimensions and five sentence frames that name shape more often than colour or texture; conclusions therefore rest on comparisons between corpora that share the frames, and counterbalancing the frames would remove this asymmetry. The models are small (3.4M to 25.6M parameters) and were trained with one recipe and five seeds per condition, and we do not know how the effects scale. Several conditions, including Colour stable corpora, exist only as fixed files, which their models memorised. Novel test nouns had untrained input embeddings, because a tie between input and output embeddings was lost on the training hardware, so the noun results rest on nouns from training. That Regular models predicted a trained kind's shape from its noun is an inference we did not test directly. The clustering statistic counts repeated labels, not referents, and effective clustering rests on 500 pairs coded by one person, with a language model as a consistency check. All corpora are English. The pretrained-model analysis does not manipulate the arrangement of their training data, so it cannot show that clustering causes what it reports.

\section*{Ethics statement}
The corpus analysis is a secondary analysis of archived, de-identified transcripts (CHILDES, CABank) used under their terms; no data were collected from people, and no transcript excerpts are reproduced. Code, corpora, results and all trained models will be released; anonymised copies are provided as supplementary material.
