\title{Whether Small Transformers Copy a Feature from Context\\Depends on the Noun's Training History}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A language model can predict a word's properties by copying them from the preceding text or by recalling what it has stored about the word. We ask what in a single word's training history decides which. We trained 150 small transformers on an artificial language describing objects by shape, colour and texture, and controlled how often 40 new nouns occurred, with which shapes, and how their sentences were arranged. Never-seen nouns copied a shape from a preceding sentence, but mainly because their input embeddings were untrained. Nouns met one sentence at a time stopped copying within about 100 occurrences, whatever they had been seen with. Nouns met in short bursts that shared a shape, with the shape changing between bursts, kept copying: 3.4 nats more than the same sentences spaced out (pre-registered) and 2.9 nats more after 1,000 occurrences than bursts with a fixed shape (exploratory). A stored shape did not itself reduce copying, and the copying was not tied to the noun. In these models, copying persisted where a word's own earlier mentions had been the only source of its current shape. In pretrained Pythia models, replacing nouns' embeddings had no consistent effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a language model reads \emph{The car was red. The car was}, it can predict \emph{red} because the context says so, or predict a typical car colour from what it has stored. Which source it relies on matters for how models handle documents that contradict their knowledge \citep{longpre2021,xie2024}, for how they learn new words and tokens, and for how we interpret probes that use invented words. Work on in-context and in-weights learning shows that the balance depends on the training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning from context; common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights \citep{chan2022,chan2025}. Inside pretrained models, mechanisms that copy from the context compete with mechanisms that recall stored facts \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. These results describe populations of items. We ask the same question about a single word: \emph{what in a word's own training history decides whether a model copies its features from the context?}

Three accounts are plausible, and they make different predictions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Memory competition.} A model copies until it has stored something about the word; stored knowledge then competes with the context. Words with a stored feature should stop copying sooner than words without one.
\item \textbf{Representation.} Copying for new words reflects their unusual, untrained embeddings. Once a word's embedding looks like those of familiar words, copying should stop, whatever the word was seen with.
\item \textbf{Useful context.} A model learns, word by word, whether copying has paid off. A word should keep being copied if, in training, its own earlier mentions predicted its features and memory could not.
\end{itemize}
Natural data cannot separate these, because frequent words are both familiar and well known. Invented (nonce) words, the usual probe \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}, are new in both respects at once, and conclusions drawn with them can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}.

We therefore built the separation into training. Small transformers learned an artificial language describing objects by shape, colour and texture, into which we inserted 40 new nouns. For each noun we set how often it occurred (from never to 10,000 sentences), which shapes it occurred with, and whether its sentences came one at a time or in short bursts (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). We measured how strongly each model copied a shape named in one sentence into the next mention of the noun. A first pre-registered experiment tested the memory and representation accounts; its results pointed to the useful-context account, which a second pre-registered experiment tested. We then asked whether the effect of a word's representation carries over to pretrained models.

The results favour the useful-context account. New nouns copied mainly because of their untrained embeddings. Familiar nouns met one sentence at a time stopped copying quickly, whether or not they had a stored shape. Only nouns whose earlier mentions had been the sole source of their current shape kept copying, and what they kept was a noun-specific tendency to copy, not a link between the noun and its shape. Our claims are about transformers, although we note parallels with children's word learning in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}; it depends on task diversity \citep{raventos2023}, favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023}. Its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}, and in small transformers stored bigram statistics are learned quickly and in-context ones later, through an induction head \citep{bietti2023}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. We manipulate frequency, stored knowledge and arrangement separately for single words within one model.

\textbf{Copying and recall.} Language models have long gained from copying recent words, through caches and pointers \citep{kuhn1990,grave2017,merity2017}, because words recur in bursts: a word that has occurred once is far more likely to occur again \citep{church1995,church2000}, a regularity that human memory also tracks \citep{anderson1991}. Pretrained models answer from memory more for frequent entities \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023,razeghi2022} and resist contexts that contradict what they know \citep{longpre2021,xie2024,du2024}; copying and recall compete inside the network \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}.

\textbf{New words and new tokens.} Rare and new words have poor representations \citep{schick2020}, under-trained tokens behave abnormally \citep{land2024}, and new embeddings are often initialised at the mean of existing ones \citep{hewitt2021}. Lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and when language models acquire a word depends mainly on its frequency \citep{chang2022}. Children's word learning offers related evidence on shape \citep{landau1988,smith2002}, on expectations about how features vary \citep{kemp2007,dewar2010} and on the timing of exposures \citep{childers2002,schwab2016}, to which we return in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by an invented noun (\emph{wug}, \emph{dax}, \dots), with 14 exemplars per kind. An exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten invented words, and is described in sentences such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture) or \emph{Look at the wug. It is zibby and lavo}. In \textbf{stable-shape} corpora each kind keeps one shape, so a kind's shape can be stored. In \textbf{shifting-shape} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass through the training text and keeps it within the pass, so its current shape can come only from the context. In both, sentences about a kind come in runs of four; a control corpus uses random order. Every word is one token.

\textbf{Models.} Decoder-only transformers with pre-layer normalisation and learned position embeddings, with 3.4M, 11.0M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers), read the text once in non-overlapping windows of 128 tokens. Input and output embeddings are untied, so a noun's input embedding (its \emph{row}) carries everything the model has learned about the noun as an input. We trained ten seeds of each size (Appendix~\ref{app:C}).

\textbf{Controlling a noun's history.} We added 40 new nouns to each corpus. Four were never used in training (\emph{unseen}). Each of the others had one history (Table~\ref{tab:design}) at a set number of exposures, counted in sentences, with three nouns per history and exposure level, rotated over nouns across seeds. A noun's sentences were spread evenly from 5\% to 95\% of training, and each lay wholly inside one window. Presented \emph{spaced}, a noun has one sentence per window; presented in \emph{bursts}, four consecutive sentences in one window. Every exposure was verified from the tokens, and the corpora matched checksums recorded before training.

\textbf{Two experiments.} Experiment~1 (90 models) crossed fixed-, changing- and no-shape nouns, all spaced, with 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, in stable-shape, shifting-shape and random-order corpora. Fixed- and changing-shape nouns appeared in five sentence frames, and no-shape nouns in a frame that names no shape (\emph{The \textit{noun} is \textit{colour} and \textit{texture}}). Experiment~2 (60 models) crossed the same three histories with spaced and burst presentation at 100 and 1,000 exposures, in stable-shape and shifting-shape corpora. To match frames, fixed- and changing-shape nouns appeared only in the test frame, and no-shape nouns in that frame without the shape (\emph{A \textit{noun} is a \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}). The experiments also differ in how much of the text the new nouns fill (62\% and 8\% of windows), so we compare conditions only within an experiment.

\textbf{Measures.} A test item gives one sentence about a noun naming a shape $s$, then the start of the next mention, \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the model's log probability $L$ of each of the ten shape words at the next position (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). The \textbf{copy score} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes, leaving out the noun's own shape if it has one; $s$ is never that shape. It is in nats: a score of 0 means the context's shape is no more likely than the others, and 3.4 means it is about 30 times as likely as the (geometric) average other shape. Each noun gets 36 items. In a \textbf{control}, the query names a different noun with the same history; if copying is tied to the noun, the control score should be lower. \textbf{Knowledge} is the preference for a noun's own shape after an unrelated sentence. We also score unseen nouns with a \textbf{typical embedding}: the mean of the trained kinds' rows plus a random offset of typical length.

\textbf{Analysis and registration.} The seed is the unit of replication: we average each condition over the three model sizes within a seed and test across ten seeds, with Holm correction within each experiment, and count a prediction as supported only if its direction also holds in two of the three sizes. Both experiments were registered before any model was trained (Experiment~1 before its corpora were built). Below, \prereg{} marks pre-registered tests and \explor{} exploratory analyses. Appendix~\ref{app:map} maps every claim to its registered hypothesis and lists all registered tests, including those not discussed here.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{New nouns copy because their embeddings are untrained}\label{sec:rows}
In stable-shape models of Experiment~1, unseen nouns had a copy score of 4.53 nats [95\% CI 3.54,~5.52], but $-$0.33 when given a typical embedding (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left). Earlier experiments in the same language had found the same: arranging text in runs raised an index of copying for unseen nouns by 11.08 nats [9.57,~12.59] over random order, and by only 3.00 [1.79,~4.21] with typical embeddings (Appendix~\ref{app:L}). To see what about an unseen embedding matters, we changed only the rows of unseen nouns in the Experiment~1 models. Scaling a row to the trained kinds' size, keeping its direction, lowered the score from 4.53 to 4.00 (difference 0.52 [0.20,~0.85]\prereg), almost entirely in the smallest models. A random direction of the same size gave the same score (4.09\explor), but the trained kinds' mean row removed copying ($-$0.44\explor). Copying for unseen nouns thus depends on the row lying away from where trained rows lie. Probes with new words therefore measure the new word's representation as much as anything learned about context.

\subsection{Nouns met one at a time stop copying, whatever they store}
Experiment~1 tested the memory account. Fixed-shape nouns stored their shape: their preference for it after an unrelated sentence reached 8.3 to 9.5 nats at 1,000 exposures in the three corpora\prereg. If stored knowledge drives copying out, their copy score should have fallen faster than that of no-shape nouns. It did not\prereg{} (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left). In stable-shape models every familiar noun lost copying at the same pace: 3.6 to 4.5 nats at 10 exposures, 0.2 to 0.9 at 100, and within 0.4 nats of zero from 1,000 on. Fixed-shape nouns stayed within 0.4 nats of changing-shape nouns at every exposure\explor, and no-shape nouns at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures ended close to unseen nouns with a typical embedding (0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52] above them, a reliable difference that is still within the registered 1-nat equivalence bound\prereg). In random-order corpora no noun copied\explor, so copying is learned from the runs in the text.

This fits the representation account: once a noun was familiar, it stopped copying whether or not it had a stored shape. But in Experiment~1 a familiar noun never recurred within a window, whereas the trained kinds did, and no-shape nouns never appeared in the test frame. Either could explain the loss of copying. Experiment~2 removed both differences.

\subsection{Bursts with changing shapes keep copying alive}
In Experiment~2 the same histories came either spaced or in bursts. The useful-context account predicts that only changing-shape bursts, where a noun's previous sentence gives its current shape and memory cannot, keep copying. That is what we found (Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, left; Table~\ref{tab:key}). Changing-shape bursts scored 3.39 nats [2.69,~4.09] more than changing-shape spaced nouns\prereg, as much at 1,000 exposures (3.35) as at 100 (3.43). After 1,000 exposures they still scored 3.85 nats, against 0.5 to 1.0 for every other familiar history.

Bursts also give a noun a quarter as many training windows as spaced sentences. Fixed-shape bursts control for this: they have the same frame, presentation and number of windows, but their shape never changes, so memory can supply it. Changing-shape bursts scored 1.73 nats [1.25,~2.20] more than fixed-shape bursts\explor, and 2.86 [1.99,~3.74] more at 1,000 exposures.

Recurrence on its own did little. No-shape bursts scored 0.80 nats [0.47,~1.13] more than no-shape spaced nouns\prereg, but the gain was confined to 100 exposures (1.55) and gone by 1,000 (0.05 [$-$0.30,~0.40]). Fixed-shape bursts also scored more than fixed-shape spaced nouns (1.69 [1.29,~2.08]\prereg), but at 100 exposures bursts also left the stored shape weaker (3.0 against 5.4 nats), so this difference cannot be attributed to recurrence; we had registered that it would be read only if stored knowledge was equal. At 1,000 exposures, when it was, the gain was 0.47 [0.27,~0.66]\explor.

\subsection{A stored shape does not itself reduce copying}
We had predicted that fixed-shape nouns, presented spaced, would copy less than no-shape nouns, and they did, by 1.02 nats [0.76,~1.27]\prereg. But the comparison is confounded. No-shape nouns were trained in \emph{A \textit{noun} is a \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}, so at the test position they led the model to expect a colour word, whereas every other familiar noun led it to expect a shape (Appendix~\ref{app:N}). Fixed- and changing-shape nouns presented spaced share frame, presentation and expected word class, and differ only in whether one shape is stored. Their copy scores were the same ($-$0.02 [$-$0.16,~0.11]\explor), within $\pm$0.3 nats. A stored shape mattered only together with bursts: fixed-shape bursts, whose earlier sentences predicted a shape that memory also supplied, lost copying, and changing-shape bursts did not.

\subsection{What the model learns is a noun-specific switch, not a link}
If changing-shape bursts taught the model to bind a noun to its current shape, copying should be weaker when the context sentence is about a different noun. It was not. In stable-shape models, the copy score with a context about the same noun exceeded the score with a context about another noun of the same history by at most 0.07 nats in Experiment~2, and 0.14 in Experiment~1\explor. The model copied the most recent shape after the queried noun whichever noun the context described: the noun's history set whether it copied, not what it copied. A registered search for heads that carry the shape did not find them. Ablating the heads with the highest copying and prefix-matching scores, nearly all in the first layer, removed a mean of 22\% of unseen nouns' copy score, against a predicted 50\%, and no more than ablating random heads\prereg{} (Appendix~\ref{app:M}).

Shifting-shape corpora, where the trained kinds' own shapes can be recovered only from context, behaved differently (Figure~\ref{fig:a10}, right\explor). There, part of copying was tied to the noun (0.48 nats for unseen nouns, 1.45 for changing-shape bursts at 1,000 exposures), all bursts kept more copying (fixed shape 3.57, no shape 2.09 at 1,000, although bursts again weakened stored shapes), and changing-shape bursts copied more than unseen nouns (6.82 against 5.02). What a noun's history teaches therefore depends on what the rest of the corpus teaches about stored features.

\subsection{Pretrained models}\label{sec:pythia}
Finally, we asked whether the representation effect carries over to pretrained models, where real nouns have long histories. In Pythia models from 1B to 12B parameters \citep{biderman2023}, we read 22 pairs of one-token nouns: one with a typical colour (\emph{sky}, \emph{leaf}, \emph{bone}) and one without (\emph{truck}, \emph{hat}, \emph{dog}), matched on frequency in the Pile \citep{gao2020,liu2024infinigram} and on count or mass use. Each was read in frames such as \emph{The \textit{noun} was \textit{colour}. The \textit{noun} was}, and the copy score was computed over 11 colour words. The models did store typical colours: with no context, colour-typical nouns preferred their colour by 1.3 to 1.7 nats. Human colour norms \citep{paik2021} agreed with our coding for 15 of the 16 colour-typical nouns they cover.

Real nouns copied 1.9 to 2.7 nats of colour (Figure~\ref{fig:pythia}). We had predicted that replacing a noun's embedding with a random one of untrained scale would raise copying, more than replacing it with a typical one, and that colour-typical nouns would copy less. None of these held\prereg: the replacements raised copying in some models and lowered it in others, by up to 0.4 nats, and colour-typical and colour-variable nouns did not differ reliably. Most changes were also not small enough to count as zero, so the result is inconsistent rather than null. Changing only the size of a replaced embedding changed copying by less than 0.01 nats. The untrained-embedding effect of the small models did not transfer to these models and this probe.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{Which account?} The memory account failed: a stored shape neither sped up the loss of copying nor, with word class matched, lowered it. The representation account explains why unseen nouns copy and why familiar nouns met one at a time stop, but not by itself why changing-shape bursts still copy after 1,000 exposures. The useful-context account explains all three. In our reading, the model learns for each noun whether the shape just mentioned has predicted the next one when memory could not. Changing-shape bursts are the only history in which that was true, and the only one in which copying lasted.

\textbf{What is expected and what is not.} That a model copies where copying lowers its loss is expected. Three things are not. Recurrence alone did not keep copying: no-shape bursts recur as often as changing-shape bursts but lost copying by 1,000 exposures. A stored shape did not compete with the context unless the context also repeated it. And the model never tied the copied shape to the noun, although in training the copied shape always belonged to that noun. What it learned is closer to a per-word switch on a general copying operation, set by the word's embedding. This is the population-level finding of \citet{chan2022}, that bursty items whose meaning changes favour learning in context, acting on single words within one model.

\textbf{Implications.} For probes with invented words, an unseen token's representation can produce most of an effect, so effects should be checked with representations that look familiar. For new tokens, initialising embeddings at the mean of existing ones \citep{hewitt2021} may suppress the very copying that lets a model learn them from context. For knowledge conflicts, our results suggest that entities whose attributes are stable within documents but vary across them may be the ones a model most readily takes from context. In Pythia we found no such link for colour, and testing it directly needs measures of each word's burstiness in pretraining.

\textbf{Analogies with children.} Two findings echo research on how children learn words, which we note as analogies only. Changing-shape bursts have the structure of the boxes in \citet{dewar2010}: uniform within a box, varying between boxes. After one draw from a new box, 9-month-olds expected the next to match, an overhypothesis in the sense of \citet{kemp2007}. And at 100 exposures, spaced nouns stored their shape more strongly than nouns met in bursts, while bursts kept the context in use. Children likewise retain words and categories better from spaced than from massed exposures \citep{childers2002,vlach2008}, although they learn new labels better when the labels are repeated in successive sentences \citep{schwab2016}.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three features and a few sentence frames, and the models are small and trained with one recipe; most effects were largest in the smallest models. We tested copying only in the frame and feature that changing-shape nouns were trained on, with the context one sentence back. The other new nouns in a corpus may affect a noun's copying, and we did not train models with a single history. The head search found no specific heads, so the mechanism is unknown. Planned power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57, below the target of 0.8. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs, three frames and colour only, and did not separate contexts that agree or conflict with a noun's typical colour. Earlier experiments in this language were registered in stages, some after related results were known (Appendix~\ref{app:A}).
