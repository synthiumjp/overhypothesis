\title{Whether Small Transformers Copy a Feature from Context\\Depends on the Noun's Training History}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Small transformers trained on text in which nearby sentences describe the same kind learn to copy a feature from one sentence into the next. Probes of this use nonce nouns, which have untrained representations. In two registered experiments with 150 models (3.4M to 25.6M parameters) trained on an artificial language, we controlled how often and how 40 nonce nouns appeared. With stable shapes, unseen nouns copied a shape from the preceding sentence, but mostly because of their untrained input rows. In the first experiment, familiar nouns whose sentences came one per window stopped copying within about 100 exposures. Nouns whose sentences came in runs that shared a shape, with the shape changing between runs, kept copying: 3.4 nats more than the same history presented singly (registered), and 2.9 nats more at 1,000 exposures than runs with a fixed shape (exploratory). With stable shapes, copying did not depend on whether the context sentence was about the same noun. In pretrained Pythia models, replacing nouns' input rows had no consistent effect on copying colour. In small models trained with stable shapes, a familiar noun kept triggering copying only if, in training, its own earlier sentences had been the sole source of its current shape.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A language model can take a word's properties from the text around it or from what it has stored about the word. Which it does depends on its training data. Classes that occur in bursts, or whose meaning changes, favour learning in context, and common, stable classes favour learning stored in the weights, both across training distributions and between rare and common classes within one model \citep{chan2022,chan2025}. In pretrained models, mechanisms that copy from the context compete with recall from memory \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. We ask how this plays out for single words: as a model sees a noun more often, when does it stop copying the noun's features from the context, and what about the noun's training decides this?

Probes with nonce words cannot answer this alone, because a nonce word is new in two ways: it has nothing stored about it, and its representation is untrained or unusual \citep{schick2020,land2024}. Conclusions drawn with nonce words can depend on how the new word is represented \citep{kim2022lexical,cacioli2026me}, and we found this in our own earlier experiments (Section~\ref{sec:background}): small transformers carried a shape from one mention of an unseen noun to the next, but most of that carry vanished when the noun was given an input embedding typical of trained nouns.

We therefore controlled, for each of 40 nonce nouns, how many times it appeared in training, with what shapes, and how its sentences were arranged, and registered the hypotheses before training (Appendices~\ref{app:M} and~\ref{app:N}). The second experiment tests two confounds found in the first. In models trained with stable shapes, we find:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Unseen nouns copy because their rows are unusual.} A typical input row removed most of their carry; so did moving the row to the trained nouns' mean (Experiment~1; row analysis).
\item \textbf{Familiar nouns presented singly stop copying.} By 100 exposures carry was under 1 nat, whether a noun had been seen with a fixed shape, a changing shape or no shape (Experiment~1).
\item \textbf{Nouns whose earlier sentences were the only source of their shape keep copying.} With sentences in runs that shared a shape, and the shape changing between runs, carry stayed high at 1,000 exposures. Runs with a fixed shape, or no shape, did not keep it, and with frame and expected word class matched a stored shape did not lower carry (Experiment~2).
\item \textbf{The copying is not specific to the noun.} Carry was the same when the context sentence was about another noun with the same history.
\end{enumerate}
In pretrained Pythia models, replacing real nouns' input rows had no consistent effect on how much colour they copied (Experiment~3). Our claims are about transformers, not children.

\section{Related work}
\textbf{In-context and in-weights learning.} In-context learning emerges when items occur in bursts, more when their meanings change, and trades off against learning in the weights \citep{chan2022}; it depends on task diversity \citep{raventos2023}, favours rare classes and can fade as stored solutions take over \citep{chan2025,singh2023}, and its onset has been traced to induction heads \citep{olsson2022,reddy2024}. Resetting embeddings during training keeps in-context use alive for rare and unseen tokens \citep{anand2025}, and in synthetic biographies the most frequent entities shift from context to stored knowledge \citep{kim2026training}. We vary, for single words within one model, how often a word occurs, what is stored about it and whether its own contexts are informative.

\textbf{Context against memory.} Models answer from memory more for frequent entities \citep{mallen2023,kandpal2023,razeghi2022}, resist contexts that contradict what they know \citep{longpre2021,xie2024,du2024}, and copying and recall compete inside the network \citep{yu2023,jin2024,ortu2024}. Here frequency is observed, not manipulated.

\textbf{Nonce words, rare words and new tokens.} Nonce words are the usual test of generalisation to a new item \citep{thrush2020,misra2023}. Rare and new words have poor representations \citep{schick2020}, under-trained tokens behave abnormally \citep{land2024}, lexical exposure can inflate apparent generalisation \citep{kim2022lexical}, and controlling how often an item occurs shows how frequency shapes what transformers learn \citep{wei2021}. We control exposure down to the window in which each occurrence is read.

\section{Setting and earlier results}\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Language and models.} Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by a nonce noun, with 14 exemplars per kind. Each exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each one of ten nonce words, and is described in one of several frames, such as \emph{A wug is a zibby lavo shorfi thing} (shape, colour, texture; Appendix~\ref{app:I}). In \textbf{Regular} corpora each kind keeps one shape. In \textbf{Reassigned} corpora a kind gets a new shape in every pass through the corpus and keeps it within the pass, so only the context can tell a trained noun's current shape. In \textbf{grouped} order, sentences about a kind come in runs of four; in \textbf{random} order they are shuffled. Text is read once, in non-overlapping 128-token windows. Models are pre-LayerNorm decoder-only transformers with learned position embeddings, of 3.4M, 11.0M and 25.6M parameters (4 to 8 layers; Appendix~\ref{app:C}). Every artificial word is one token.

\textbf{Carry.} A test item gives one context sentence about a noun, naming shape $s$, then the query \emph{A \textit{noun} is a}, and reads the log probability $L$ of each of the ten shapes at the next position (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). \textbf{Carry} is $L(s)$ minus the mean of $L$ over the other shapes, excluding the noun's own shape $s^*$ (a placebo shape for nouns without one); $s$ is never $s^*$. Each noun gets 36 items, each of the nine shapes other than $s^*$ four times. A carry of 3.4 nats means the context's shape is about 30 times as probable as the geometric mean of the other shapes. In a \textbf{control}, the query names another noun with the same history, and \textbf{noun-specific carry} is carry minus control carry (on the 32 items where the context shape is not the other noun's own). \textbf{Knowledge} is $L(s^*)$ minus the mean over the other nine shapes after an unrelated sentence.

\textbf{Earlier results.} In registered experiments with 120 models (Appendix~\ref{app:A}), grouped training raised a related index of carry for unseen nouns over random order by 11.08 nats [9.57,~12.59]. But the unseen nouns' input embeddings had never been trained, and when the same nouns were given an embedding typical of trained nouns (their mean plus a random offset of typical length; compare initialising new embeddings at the mean, \citealp{hewitt2021}), the effect shrank to 3.00 nats [1.79,~4.21] (exploratory; Appendix~\ref{app:L}). So these probes measured arrangement together with the test noun's representation. Below, input and output embeddings are untied by design.

\section{Experiment 1: exposure and history}\label{sec:exp1}
\textbf{Design.} We added 40 nonce nouns to three base corpora (Regular grouped, Regular random and Reassigned grouped). In every model they fill 13 cells: three histories, each at 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 exposures, three nouns per cell, plus four unseen nouns. A \emph{fixed-shape} noun always appears with its own shape $s^*$; an \emph{uninformative} noun with a new shape every sentence, drawn from the corpus's shapes; a \emph{shape-absent} noun only in a frame that names no shape (\emph{The \textit{noun} is \textit{colour} and \textit{texture}}, which trained kinds also use). A trained kind appears in about 60,000 to 100,000 sentences. Each familiarity sentence lies wholly inside one window, at most one per window, between runs in grouped corpora; a noun's exposures are evenly spaced from 5\% to 95\% of training. A checker verified every exposure from the tokens, and the corpora matched checksums recorded before training. We trained 90 models (three sizes, ten seeds, three base corpora). The experiment was registered before any of its corpora was built.

\textbf{Analysis.} The seed is the unit of replication: we average cell means over the three sizes within each seed and test over ten seeds, with \citet{holm1979} correction within each experiment. A hypothesis is supported only if its direction also holds at two of the three sizes. Hypotheses are numbered as in the registrations.

\textbf{Results.} Fixed-shape nouns learned their shape (knowledge at 1,000 exposures 8.3 to 9.5 nats in the three corpora; M1 passed). In grouped Regular models, unseen nouns carried 4.53 nats [3.54,~5.52], but only \textminus{}0.33 with a typical row. Familiar nouns lost carry for every history (Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, left): 3.6 to 4.5 nats at 10 exposures, 0.16 to 0.86 at 100, and within 0.4 nats of zero from 1,000 on. Grouped models carried more than random ones for unseen nouns (4.68 [3.71,~5.65]), and in random order carry was near zero for every noun.

We predicted that carry would fall faster for fixed-shape than for shape-absent nouns (H30). It did not (slope difference 0.16 nats per decade [0.01,~0.30], the wrong way). Fixed-shape nouns' stored preference for $s^*$ rose by 2.97 nats per decade (H32), yet they carried within 0.4 nats of uninformative nouns at every exposure (exploratory). Familiar shape-absent nouns at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures carried 0.33 nats [0.13,~0.52] more than unseen nouns with a typical row; the difference was reliable, and the two were also equivalent within the registered 1-nat bound (H31). In Reassigned models (exploratory), familiar nouns kept more carry (shape-absent: about 2 nats at 1,000 and 10,000 exposures; Figure~\ref{fig:carry}, middle). Carry was not specific to the noun: in every Regular cell, noun-specific carry was at most 0.14 nats (Table~\ref{tab:N10}; cells not shown there were at most 0.08).

A registered head search did not localise carry. We ablated the heads with the highest product of copying and prefix-matching scores, the signature of induction heads \citep{olsson2022}; no head showed clear prefix matching, so these were copying heads, nearly all in the first layer. Ablation removed a mean proportion of 0.22 [0.07,~0.36] of unseen carry, against a registered 0.5, and not reliably more than random heads (H33 not supported). In Reassigned models it cut trained kinds' carry by 7.41 nats (H34 supported), but random heads cut it by 6.91, so the cut is not specific (Appendix~\ref{app:M}).

\textbf{Two confounds.} A blind review of this experiment (Appendix~\ref{app:N}) noted two differences between familiar nouns and trained kinds other than familiarity. A familiar noun never recurred within a window, while trained kinds came in runs, where an earlier sentence predicts the next. And shape-absent nouns never appeared in the test frame.

\section{Experiment 2: recurrence and useful context}\label{sec:exp2}
\textbf{Design.} The 40 nonce nouns were reassigned to 12 cells, three nouns each, plus four unseen nouns: three histories $\times$ two presentations $\times$ 100 or 1,000 exposures (Table~\ref{tab:design2}; Figure~\ref{fig:1}b). Presented \emph{singly}, a noun has one sentence per window. Presented in \emph{runs}, it has four consecutive sentences in one window, so runs and singles are matched in sentences but runs give a noun a quarter as many windows. Fixed and rotating nouns now appear only in the test frame, and shape-absent nouns in a frame that begins like it but names no shape (\emph{A \textit{noun} is a \textit{colour} \textit{texture} thing}), which trained kinds also use in one sentence in fifteen. With fewer cells and exposures, the nonce nouns fill about 8\% of windows, against 62\% in Experiment~1. Everything else is as in Experiment~1. We trained 60 models on Regular grouped (confirmatory) and Reassigned grouped (exploratory) corpora, three sizes and ten seeds. The experiment was registered after its corpora were built and checksummed and before any of them was used for training.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} We registered: runs and singles differ for shape-absent nouns (H40) and fixed nouns (H41), each two-sided with equivalence bounds of $\pm$0.3 nats; rotating runs carry more than rotating singles (H42); fixed singles carry less than shape-absent singles (H43); shape-absent singles differ from unseen nouns with a typical row (H44, two-sided, $\pm$0.3). A check (K1) required fixed nouns' knowledge to be equivalent within $\pm$1 nat between runs and singles; otherwise H41 could reflect a change in what is stored rather than recurrence. The 100- and 1,000-exposure cells are pooled. Splits by exposure, matched contrasts and the control are exploratory.

\textbf{Runs that made the context the only source kept carry.} Unseen nouns carried 6.35 nats [5.59,~7.12] (M2 passed). Rotating nouns in runs carried 3.39 nats [2.69,~4.09] more than rotating nouns presented singly (H42 supported; Figure~\ref{fig:a10}): 3.43 at 100 exposures and 3.35 at 1,000. Runs and singles differ in the number of windows, but rotating runs also carried more than fixed runs, which are matched in frame, presentation and windows: 1.73 nats [1.25,~2.20] pooled, and 2.86 [1.99,~3.74] at 1,000 exposures (Table~\ref{tab:N9}). At 1,000 exposures rotating runs still carried 3.85 nats, against 0.50 to 1.03 for every other familiar cell (Table~\ref{tab:N2}).

\textbf{Recurrence alone helped less.} Shape-absent nouns in runs carried 0.80 nats [0.47,~1.13] more than in singles (H40, ``above''): 1.55 at 100 exposures and 0.05 [\textminus{}0.30,~0.40] at 1,000. Fixed nouns in runs carried 1.69 [1.29,~2.08] more (H41, ``above''), but K1 failed: at 100 exposures runs weakened the stored shape (knowledge 3.0 against 5.4 nats; Table~\ref{tab:N3}). By the registered rule we do not read H41 as a recurrence effect. At 1,000 exposures, where knowledge was equivalent, the difference was 0.47 [0.27,~0.66].

\textbf{A stored shape did not lower carry once expectations were matched.} Fixed singles carried 1.02 nats [0.76,~1.27] less than shape-absent singles (H43 supported), and shape-absent singles carried 1.42 [1.12,~1.73] more than unseen nouns with a typical row (H44, ``above'', not equivalent; 2.48 at 100 exposures, 0.37 [0.15,~0.59] at 1,000). But shape-absent nouns, trained in a frame where a colour follows \emph{is a}, expected a colour rather than a shape at the test position, whereas every other familiar noun expected a shape (Table~\ref{tab:N3}). Fixed and rotating singles share frame, presentation and expected word class, and differ only in whether one shape is stored; their carry was the same ($-$0.02 [$-$0.16,~0.11]; Table~\ref{tab:N9}). H43 thus reflects what the noun predicts at the test position, not a stored shape.

\textbf{Not specific to the noun.} In Regular models, noun-specific carry was at most 0.07 nats in every cell, including rotating runs (Table~\ref{tab:N10}): the model copied the context's shape after the query noun whichever noun the context was about.

\textbf{Reassigned models (exploratory).} Run effects were larger (H40 1.71, H42 4.67), rotating runs at 1,000 exposures carried more than unseen nouns (6.82 against 5.02), and fixed and shape-absent runs also kept carry (3.57 and 2.09), though for fixed nouns runs again weakened the stored shape. Here part of carry was noun-specific (0.48 for unseen nouns, 1.45 for rotating runs at 1,000). Overall levels differed between the experiments (unseen carry 6.35 against 4.53 nats in Regular models, 5.02 against 6.85 in Reassigned ones). The trained kinds' text follows the same rules except for the frame without a shape, but the corpora were drawn afresh and differ in the nonce nouns' frames, histories and share of the text, so we compare conditions only within an experiment.

\section{Row analysis}\label{sec:exp3}
A noun enters the model only through its input row. In Experiment~1's grouped models we replaced only input rows and scored carry again; unchanged rows reproduced the original scores. In Regular models, scaling an unseen noun's row to the trained kinds' mean norm, keeping its direction, lowered carry from 4.53 to 4.00 nats (difference 0.52 [0.20,~0.85]; H49 supported), driven by the 3.4M models ($-$1.79; +0.27 and $-$0.05 in the larger models). A random direction at that norm gave the same carry (4.09), and the trained kinds' mean row removed it (\textminus{}0.44). Shrinking a familiar shape-absent noun's row (10,000 exposures) to the unseen nouns' norm raised its carry only from \textminus{}0.05 to 0.53. So the norm explains little; carry needs a row away from where trained rows lie. In Reassigned models (exploratory) rescaling raised unseen carry (by 0.27), and shrinking a familiar row raised its carry from 1.86 to 4.16 (Table~\ref{tab:N5}).

\section{Experiment 3: pretrained models}\label{sec:exp4}
\textbf{Design.} This experiment asks only whether the row result transfers: do pretrained models copy more for nouns given an untrained-like row, or less for nouns with a typical colour? It does not manipulate history. We used Pythia deduplicated models \citep{biderman2023} (final checkpoints; 1B to 12B primary, 70M and 160M descriptive, 410M pilot). Forty-four one-token nouns formed 22 pairs of a colour-typical noun (e.g., \emph{sky}, \emph{leaf}, \emph{bone}) and a colour-variable noun (e.g., \emph{truck}, \emph{hat}, \emph{dog}), matched on count/mass type and on frequency in the Pile \citep{gao2020}, counted with infini-gram \citep{liu2024infinigram}. Text-trained models' colour knowledge follows how colours are reported in text \citep{paik2021}, so we also checked that typical colours were stored. Two passes of an AI coder with a fixed prompt rated colour typicality before any model was run (weighted $\kappa$ = 0.96). Each noun was read in three frames such as \emph{The \textit{noun} was \textit{colour}. The \textit{noun} was}, with the 11 basic colour terms that are one token; carry is the mean over frames and ordered colour pairs of $\log p(c_1 \mid \text{context } c_1) - \log p(c_1 \mid \text{context } c_2)$, renormalised over colours. We replaced only each noun's input row, with a random direction at the norm of an initial row (\emph{atypical\_init}) or a typical row. We registered that carry would be higher with atypical\_init rows than with original (H45) or typical rows (H46), lower for colour-typical nouns (H47), and that the H45 increase would differ between groups (H48); each needed Holm-corrected significance, within each model, in four of the five.

\textbf{Results.} Colour-typical nouns did prefer their colour with no context (by 1.3 to 1.7 nats, for 82 to 95\% of nouns; Table~\ref{tab:N12}). With their own rows, nouns carried 1.89 to 2.70 nats of colour. No hypothesis was supported. Atypical\_init rows changed carry by \textminus{}0.38 to +0.34 nats against original rows, Holm-significant and positive in two models (1B, 1.4B) and negative in two others; against typical rows the pattern differed (positive and significant in 1B and 6.9B, negative in 1.4B and 12B). Colour-typical and colour-variable nouns did not differ reliably (\textminus{}0.16 to 0.11), and H48 was inconclusive (Table~\ref{tab:N6}). The changes were mostly not equivalent to zero within $\pm$0.3 nats either, so the result is inconsistent, not null. Changing only the norm of a replaced row changed carry by under 0.01 nats on average in every primary model. A mixed model over the five models gave no effect of either row or of group.

\section{Discussion}
In our small transformers trained with stable shapes, whether a familiar noun triggered copying of the preceding sentence's shape depended on its training history. Nouns presented singly stopped within about 100 exposures in Experiment~1, whatever they had been seen with. Nouns presented in runs that shared a shape, with the shape changing between runs, still carried 3.85 nats at 1,000 exposures; runs with a fixed shape or no shape carried about 1 nat. Our reading is that copying persisted where the noun's own earlier sentences were the only reliable source of its current shape. These are the conditions, bursty items whose meaning changes, that favour in-context learning for classes \citep{chan2022}; here they act on single nouns within one model. Experiment~1's apparent conclusion, that familiarity switches copying off, holds only for nouns whose contexts never made copying useful.

Two qualifications follow. First, the copying was not bound to the noun: in Regular models a noun's history decided whether the model copied the last shape, not whether it tracked that noun's shape, and only Reassigned training produced a noun-specific part. Second, the noun's history interacts with the rest of the corpus. In Reassigned models, where trained kinds teach that a stored shape can be wrong, runs kept carry even with a fixed or no shape, and overall carry differed between our two experiments.

Stored knowledge itself mattered less than we expected: with frame and expected word class matched, a stored shape did not lower carry relative to a shape that changed every sentence. Where it may matter is in runs: fixed runs, whose earlier sentences predicted a shape that memory also supplied, lost carry (4.43 to 0.99 nats from 100 to 1,000 exposures), and rotating runs lost far less (5.02 to 3.85).

Unseen nouns are not a neutral baseline. Their carry depended on rows unlike those of trained nouns, which fits the finding that untrained embeddings preserve in-context use \citep{anand2025}. Effects measured with nonce tokens should be checked with representations that look familiar. In Pythia, replacing a real noun's row did not change colour copying consistently, and the row's norm made almost no difference, so the untrained-row effect did not transfer to these models and this probe. Whether pretrained models' copying depends on a noun's burstiness in pretraining remains to be tested.

\section*{Limitations}
The language is artificial, with 32 kinds, three dimensions and a few frames; the small models are trained with one recipe, and most effects were largest in the 3.4M models (Table~\ref{tab:N11}). We tested carry only in the frame and dimension that rotating-run nouns were trained on, one sentence back; whether it transfers to other frames, to colour or to longer gaps is untested. Runs and singles differ in the number of windows; the matched contrast with fixed runs addresses this for the main result but not for H40. The other nonce nouns may affect a noun's carry, and we did not train models with a single history. We have no mechanism: the head search found no specific heads. Expected power for a 1-nat difference in Experiment~1 was 0.57, below the planned 0.8. The pretrained test used one model family, 22 noun pairs, three frames and colour only, did not separate contexts that agree or conflict with a noun's typical colour, and its colour codes came from two passes of an AI coder, not human raters. Earlier experiments were registered in stages, some after related results were known (Appendix~\ref{app:A}). Experiments 1 and 2 were registered before training, and every registered test is reported.
