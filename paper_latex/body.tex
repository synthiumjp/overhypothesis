\begin{abstract}
Language-model training data are studied mostly for content, not arrangement. We tested arrangement with a regularity that holds across items: a learner that sees one new object called a \emph{dax} might expect the next dax to share its shape rather than its colour. In Study 1 we trained 390 small transformers on artificial languages. With sentence content held fixed, grouping the sentences about each kind raised a shape-over-colour index from 0.47 to 6.49 nats. The gain appeared only when a feature was fixed within kinds, followed whichever feature that was, and rose with clustering. Runs describing different objects of a kind taught it more selectively than runs re-describing one object, which taught the models to expect every feature to repeat. A longer window, with tokens matched, did not raise the index. On nouns from training, most of the expectation carried over to an object with another name, although the grouped corpora tied shape repetition to the name. In Study 2, object labels were more clustered in speech recorded around young children than in adult conversation or written text, although nearly half of the repetitions returned to the same object. In Study 3, a shape-over-colour expectation grew with Pythia model size. No Pythia model preferred shape over texture, and evidence that the expectation was tied to the noun was weak and depended on wording. In small transformers, local arrangement taught which feature persists from one object to the next; the models tied it to the kind only in small part.

\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
What a language model learns depends on its training data. Most work on that data asks about content: which sources, how much, and how close to a child's input \cite{warstadt2023,feng2024}. Less asks about arrangement, the order in which the same content is presented, although every training pipeline sets it when it packs documents into windows, orders them or generates synthetic text \cite{shi2024,zhao2024}. Natural sources also differ in arrangement. A word that has appeared once in a document is far more likely to appear again soon than its frequency predicts \cite{church2000}, and parents' talk to toddlers returns to the same objects in clusters \cite{slone2023}. We ask what arrangement alone, with content held fixed, changes in what a next-word learner extracts from its data.

We study a regularity that holds across items rather than within any one of them. A child who hears ``this is a dax'' about a new object will call other objects ``dax'' if they share its shape, even when they differ in size or texture \cite{landau1988}. The child has learned a fact about kinds in general: things with the same name tend to share a shape. \citet{kemp2007} called this an overhypothesis, after \citet{goodman1955}.

We give a model one description of an object of a new kind, such as \emph{A snerg is a krannish pulek skorli thing}, where the three adjectives name a shape, a colour and a texture. We then compare how strongly the model expects a second snerg to keep the shape with how strongly it expects it to keep the colour. The difference, in nats (natural-log units of probability), is our index. A positive index means the model expects shape to stay the same more than colour.

A positive index can come from two different expectations. One is tied to the kind: objects with the same name share a shape. The other is not: whatever object is described next will keep the shape of the last one. Only the first is an overhypothesis about named kinds. We separate them by changing the noun at test, which needs nouns the model can track. If the second object has a different name, the first expectation predicts a smaller preference, and the second predicts the same preference as before. The paradigm is therefore first a test of which feature a model expects to persist, and a test of an overhypothesis about named kinds only if the noun matters.

We ask four questions in three studies. Study 1 trains 390 small transformers on artificial languages in which we control both content and order, and addresses the first three questions; Study 3 returns to the third, and Studies 2 and 3 address the fourth.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Does grouping the sentences about each kind teach the fixed feature?} We predicted that the same sentences grouped into short runs by kind would give a higher index than in random order, and that random order alone would give an index above zero. We also predicted that the gain would depend on a feature being fixed within kinds and follow it, so that corpora with colour fixed would teach a preference for colour, and that the index would rise with measured clustering, so that clustering at the level we found for object labels in speech to children would already beat random order.
\item \textbf{What kind of grouping helps?} A longer training window holds more sentences about a kind without changing their order, and we predicted that it would raise the index. Nearby sentences can also describe a different object of the kind or the same object again. We predicted that different objects, which show what varies within a kind and what does not, would teach more than repeated descriptions of one object.
\item \textbf{Is the expectation tied to the noun?} We predicted that the index would be higher when the second object had the same name as the first than when it had a different name, and that larger pretrained models would show this too.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}\setcounter{enumi}{3}
\item \textbf{What do natural input and pretrained models show?} We predicted that mentions of object kinds would be clustered in speech to young children, more so than in written text, and tested, two-sided, whether it differed from adult conversation. We predicted that in Pythia models, which are trained on written text, the index would grow with size and over training, and that models trained on child-scale data would show it.
\end{enumerate}

The project used a sequence of registrations. Later ones were written after earlier stages had been examined, and some were informed by Study 1 scores later found to have been loaded incorrectly. A test is called registered only if its analysis was specified before the models concerned were trained or scored, or before the transcripts were read. Appendix A gives the dated record.

The paper makes three contributions. With content held fixed, it shows that the arrangement of training text determines how strongly small transformers learn which feature persists across objects (grouped order taught it strongly, while in random order the index was not reliably above zero), and that runs of different objects of a kind teach this more selectively than runs re-describing one object, which teach that every feature persists. It separates this persistence from an expectation tied to the noun, and shows that the models learned mainly the persistence. And it measures the same arrangement statistic in natural corpora, as a descriptive comparison. Models pretrained on written text show only a weak, partial analogue.

Our claims are about how transformers learn. We use the shape bias as a test, as \citet{ritter2017} did for image classifiers, and we treat speech to children as one natural corpus among others, not as the thing being explained.

\section{Background}
\subsection{The shape bias as a learned overhypothesis}
In the original studies, children and adults extended a novel name to objects of the same shape more readily than to objects of the same size or texture \cite{landau1988}. In toddlers, attention to shape grows as their vocabulary of object names grows \cite{gershkoffstowe2004}. Teaching 17-month-olds a few categories organised by shape made the bias appear early and sped up their learning of object names outside the laboratory \cite{smith2002}. Teaching varied exemplars of each category helped more than teaching near-identical ones \cite{perry2010}. The bias therefore looks learned, from the way named kinds vary.

\citet{kemp2007} made this precise. In their hierarchical Bayesian model each kind has its own distribution over features, and a higher level describes how variable kinds tend to be on each dimension. After a few kinds that are uniform in shape and varied in colour, the model expects a new kind to be uniform in shape after one exemplar. Associative networks trained on the structure of early noun vocabularies acquire similar expectations \cite{colunga2005}, and neural networks trained on images \cite{ritter2017} or on artificial objects \cite{feinman2018} can show a shape bias. These studies used perceptual input, or features given directly to the network. We ask whether a next-word learner acquires the regularity from descriptions in text, and what arrangement of the text it needs.

\subsection{Artificial languages and novel words}
Artificial languages let the training data be controlled exactly, so that a model's preferences can be traced to its input \cite{mccoy2020,white2021,kallini2024}. These studies vary the grammar of the language; we keep the content fixed and vary only the order of the sentences. Our test is also a form of novel-word learning. Language models acquire words largely by frequency \cite{chang2022}, can generalise a new verb from one or two sentences \cite{thrush2020} and can learn new words in context when trained to do so \cite{wang2025}, but often fail to pass a property from a concept to its subtypes when the context contains distracting information \cite{misra2023}.

\subsection{Training on speech to children}
A line of work trains language models on data closer to a child's input. A small masked model trained on 5 million words of child-directed speech learned grammar about as well as a much larger model \cite{huebner2021}. The BabyLM Challenge set limits of 10 and 100 million words \cite{warstadt2023,hu2024}. Models trained on child-directed speech capture its surface statistics but generalise questions linearly rather than hierarchically \cite{yedetore2023}. Models trained on one child's speech and video learn word classes and word-object mappings \cite{wang2023,vong2024}.

Two findings bear on arrangement. \citet{feng2024} found that the local order of child-directed speech affected what models learned, while its global order by the child's age did not. Speech to children often comes in variation sets, runs of nearby utterances that repeat and vary talk about the same thing \cite{kuntay1996}. Adults learned an artificial language better when its input contained variation sets \cite{onnis2008}, but adding generated variation sets to the training data of small language models had mixed effects \cite{haga2024}.

\subsection{Burstiness, locality and in-context learning}
Words are bursty: once a word appears, it is likely to appear again soon. Cache language models used this to predict speech \cite{kuhn1990}, and models of word counts that allow it fit text better than those that do not \cite{church1995,madsen2005}. \citet{church2000} measured this as adaptation, the chance that a word recurs in a document once it has appeared. Our clustering measure, B (Section 3.1), is close in spirit. Language models show similar effects: with an added adaptation step, a recurrent model predicts words and structures better after recent exposure \cite{vanschijndel2018}, and transformers show structural priming \cite{sinclair2022}.

In transformers, burstiness changes what is learned. \citet{chan2022} trained transformers on sequences of images and found that in-context learning emerged only when items of a class appeared in bursts. \citet{reddy2024} traced this to the abrupt formation of an induction head, and \citet{singh2023} found that in-context learning can fade as learning stored in the weights takes over. \citet{prystawski2023} showed that training on local samples from a structured distribution makes intermediate reasoning steps useful. In pretraining, putting related documents in the same window improves use of the context \cite{shi2024,zhao2024}. These studies concern what a model does with examples in its context, and they point to the value of local structure without implying a common mechanism. We ask whether training sets up an expectation about which feature persists before the context offers any evidence about it.

\subsection{Order and curricula}
Work on curricula asks whether the order of training examples matters, with mixed results. Starting small helped a recurrent network in one study \cite{elman1993} but not under more natural conditions \cite{rohde1999}; easy-to-hard ordering can speed learning \cite{bengio2009}; and at child scale, curricula based on human notions of difficulty have given inconsistent gains \cite{diehlmartinez2023}, while ordering by each example's estimated influence on the model helped \cite{schoenegger2025}. Human category learning offers a parallel. Blocked study helps learners find what members of a category share, and interleaved study what tells categories apart \cite{carvalho2014}. For children, spaced presentations helped category learning \cite{vlach2008}, but repeating a new word across successive sentences helped word learning \cite{schwab2016}.

\section{Study 1: Small models trained on an artificial language}
Study 1 asks what a transformer learns about kinds when its input contains nothing else, and how the arrangement of that input matters. We built corpora in which every regularity and the order of every sentence are under our control, and trained models small enough to train many times. Content is held fixed across orders of the same corpus and varied deliberately between corpora.

\subsection{Corpora}
Each corpus describes 32 kinds of object, each named by a novel noun (\emph{wug}, \emph{dax}, \emph{blorp} and so on), with 14 exemplars per kind. Each exemplar has a shape, a colour and a texture, each a novel word from a list of ten per dimension, and is described by one sentence in one of five frames:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A wug is a [shape] [colour] [texture] thing.}
\item \emph{Look at the wug. It is [shape] and [colour].}
\item \emph{Here is another wug. This one is [shape].}
\item \emph{The wug I showed you is [shape], but a different [colour].}
\item \emph{That is a wug because it is [shape].}
\end{enumerate}

Shape is named in all five frames, colour in three and texture in one. The frames are the same in every corpus we compare, so they cannot by themselves explain a difference between corpora, although they may interact with what is manipulated. The 448 sentences of a corpus are repeated in 26 passes, each separately shuffled: 11,648 sentences and about 102,000 words. Five corpora were generated for each condition, one per seed.

\textbf{Content.} The corpora differ in which feature, if any, stays fixed within a kind (Table 1). In \textbf{Regular} corpora every kind keeps one shape across its 14 exemplars, while colour and texture vary. In \textbf{Mixed-slot} corpora each kind keeps one word from the shape list, but for about half the kinds that word appears in the texture slot, and colour varies; no single dimension is fixed across all kinds. In \textbf{No stable feature} corpora, built from Regular, each exemplar's shape is drawn at random from the list, so nothing is fixed within a kind. In \textbf{Colour stable} corpora, also built from Regular, shape is drawn in the same way and each kind keeps one colour instead. Frame 4 is unchanged, so in Colour stable corpora it names the kind's fixed colour after the words \emph{a different}.

\textbf{Objects.} A further set of corpora separates two kinds of nearby repetition. In the Regular corpora, two sentences about a kind always describe different exemplars. In speech, nearby mentions often return to the same object. The Objects corpora keep the Regular content but give each kind 13 objects and describe each object four times, once in each of frames 1, 2, 3 and 5. Frame 4 is left out because it states that the colour differs. With seven passes, these corpora also have 11,648 sentences.

\textbf{Order.} The same sentences were arranged in several orders. In \textbf{random} order every pass is shuffled. In \textbf{grouped} order, within each pass, the sentences about each kind are shuffled, cut into runs of four, and the runs are shuffled across kinds (Figure 1). Every sentence occurs exactly as often as in random order. In \textbf{graded} orders each run is kept with a set probability and otherwise split into single sentences before shuffling, which gives intermediate levels of clustering. In the Objects corpora, a \textbf{same-object run} is the four descriptions of one object, and a \textbf{different-object run} is four different objects of one kind, one per frame. A Latin square over objects and frames ensures that every sentence is used once per pass in every order. Same-object and different-object runs were each arranged fully grouped and at an intermediate level.

\textbf{Clustering.} We measure clustering with one statistic, B, here and in Study 2. Take the sequence of mentions of object nouns in a text, and every pair of consecutive mentions that are no more than 100 words apart. P is the share of these pairs that name the same kind. $P_0$ is the same share after the sentences (or utterances) of each document are shuffled, averaged over shuffles. B = P/$P_0$, pooled over documents. For example, in \emph{Look at the dog. The dog is big. Where is the cup? Here is another dog.}, the consecutive pairs are dog--dog, dog--cup and cup--dog, so P = 1/3. B = 1 means no clustering beyond what the content predicts. Random-order Regular corpora have B = 0.9, slightly below 1, because each pass is shuffled separately: within a pass, a sentence's kind has 13 other members among 447 sentences, against 363 among 11,647 when the whole corpus is shuffled. The graded levels (Table 1) were chosen after the clustering of natural input had been measured (Study 2): 1.4 and 3.6 match B in written text (1.43) and in speech to children (3.57). They were fixed from the corpora alone before any graded-order model was trained.

\subsection{Models and training}
We trained decoder-only transformers of three sizes: 3.4 million parameters (4 layers, 4 heads, width 256), 11.0 million (6 layers, 8 heads, width 384) and 25.6 million (8 layers, 8 heads, width 512). They were trained for 5,000, 8,000 and 10,000 steps with batches of 32, 32 and 16, peak learning rates of 3 $\times$ $10^{-4}$, 2 $\times$ $10^{-4}$ and 1 $\times$ $10^{-4}$, AdamW (betas 0.9 and 0.95, weight decay 0.1), linear warm-up, cosine decay and dropout of 0.1. Each training example is a 128-token window starting at a random position in the corpus, so a window spans several sentences and attention crosses sentence boundaries; over training each model saw every position about 160 to 290 times. The tokeniser for the Regular corpora has 351 entries (314 to 339 for the other corpus families), and every artificial word is a single token. A seed sets the corpus, the initial weights and the batch order (Table 1 gives the models per condition). For the window test, models were trained with 512-token windows and a quarter of the batch size, so that every step saw the same number of tokens. All models have separate input and output embeddings, and the parameter counts include both. This was not intended: the model code ties the two, but the tie was lost on the training hardware (Appendix A). Table C1 lists every setting.

\subsection{Measuring the expectation}
Eight novel nouns that never occur in training (\emph{snerg}, \emph{twick} and so on) are used in test items. They are in the vocabulary, so each is one token, but their input embeddings were never trained; weight decay shrinks them by less than 10\% over training, so they remain distinct random vectors. With the intended tie, the same vectors would have served as the novel nouns' output weights and would have changed in training; we cannot say whether the models would then have tracked these nouns. Novel nouns keep learned knowledge about a noun out of the test, but the test then depends on the model registering that the same untrained token recurs. Section 3.5 shows that the models did not, so the informative test of the noun uses nouns from training (Appendix F). Each item gives the model one sentence in frame 1 describing an exemplar, followed by two continuations that differ in one word:

\begin{quote}
Context: \emph{A snerg is a krannish pulek skorli thing.} Repeat: \emph{A snerg is a \textbf{krannish} temil tramel thing.} New: \emph{A snerg is a \textbf{tova} temil tramel thing.}
\end{quote}

In this item the probed dimension is shape. The repeat continuation keeps the context's shape, the new one does not, and both give new values for the other two dimensions. The model scores each continuation by the sum of the log probabilities of its tokens given the context, so the difference, $\Delta$, includes the changed word and its effect on the words after it. $\Delta$ is the model's preference for repeating the probed dimension, in nats. Trained models generally prefer words they have just seen, so $\Delta$ alone mixes the dimension with copying. Our index is the difference between dimensions:

\begin{equation*}
\text{index} = \Delta_{\text{shape}} - \Delta_{\text{colour}}
\end{equation*}

Copying that affects shape and colour equally cancels. Copying that differs between dimensions does not, which is one reason every conclusion rests on comparisons between corpora tested in the same way, and why we report $\Delta$ for each dimension as well. The shape-over-texture index is defined in the same way. With one exemplar, nothing in the context shows which dimension is fixed, so differences between trained conditions come from training.

Every item is also given with a different novel noun in the continuations (\emph{A yeff is a krannish temil tramel thing}). The \textbf{noun-specific index} is the index after the same noun minus the index after a different noun. A kind-tied expectation scores above zero on it; a noun-blind one scores zero.

With 25 draws of features per noun, three probed dimensions and two continuation nouns there are 1,200 one-exemplar items. A further 2,400 items with two or four exemplars test the use of evidence in the context (Table E2). Untrained models of the same architecture, with the Regular tokeniser, give an index of 0.02 nats (SD 0.07).

\subsection{Statistics}
Each model gives one index, its mean over items. Models of the three sizes trained with the same seed share a corpus, so the five seeds are the units of replication. Registered contrasts between conditions were tested one-sided: in Amendments 1 and 2 with Welch t tests over the 15 models of each condition (rows 1, 3, 6 and 7 of Table A4), and in later ones with a linear mixed model, index $\sim$ condition + size, with a random intercept for seed, and a z test. Single conditions were compared with zero by one-sample t tests. \citet{holm1979} correction was applied within each registered family of tests. Tests reported with a Holm-corrected p, or labelled as registered, were registered; all others are exploratory. Because these tests can be overconfident with only five corpora, we also report every contrast at the seed level (Table E1): the three sizes are averaged within each seed, and the five paired differences give the mean, a t-based 95\% interval and a t test with 4 degrees of freedom. The differences and intervals given in the text are these seed-level estimates. We registered 0.2 nats as the smallest effect of interest and tested equivalence to zero against it with two one-sided tests \cite{lakens2017}. The bound was set before the loading correction, when effects were about five times smaller; on the corrected scale it is about 3\% of the grouping effect, so the equivalence tests are strict.

Every result reported here uses corrected scores; Appendix A gives the dated record and the effect of the correction.

\subsubsection*{Grouping teaches the fixed feature}
In random order, Regular models showed a shape-over-colour index of 0.47 nats, positive in 10 of 15 models. This was not reliably above zero (Holm p = .061; seed level, p = .15), nor small enough to count as equivalent to zero. The same sentences in grouped order gave 6.49 nats, higher in all five seeds and in all 15 seed-by-size pairs (difference 6.02 [5.24,~6.79], Holm p $<$~.001). The grouped index was similar at each model size (6.41, 6.83 and 6.23 nats, against 0.33, 0.71 and 0.38 in random order), and it came early: grouped models reached 80\% of their final index within 1,000 steps (the two smaller sizes) or 3,000 steps (the largest). In random-order models the index first fell, to between \textminus{}1.2 and \textminus{}1.5 nats depending on size, and reached its final level only late in training.

The gain depended on a feature that was fixed within kinds, and followed whichever feature that was. When no feature was fixed, grouping did not produce a preference for shape (grouped No stable feature models, \textminus{}0.44 nats; difference from grouped Regular 6.93 [5.47,~8.40], Holm p $<$~.001). When colour was the fixed feature, grouping produced a preference for colour. Grouped Colour stable models gave \textminus{}3.20, below grouped No stable feature models (difference 2.76 [1.99,~3.52], Holm p $<$~.001), and none of the 15 was above zero. Their components show why: they expected colour to repeat ($\Delta$ 2.39) and shape not to (\textminus{}0.81).

Mixed-slot corpora keep a fixed word for every kind but no single fixed dimension. In grouped order these models preferred shape over colour (2.12), as their corpus supports, since colour is never fixed there, but showed no reliable preference between shape and texture (0.46), each of which is fixed for about half the kinds. Grouped Regular models were above them on both indices (differences 4.37 [1.98,~6.76] and 4.89 [2.69,~7.10], Holm p $<$~.001). In random order, Regular models were above Mixed-slot models by the registered test (0.47 against \textminus{}0.25, Holm p = .032), but not reliably at the seed level (3 of 5 seeds; p = .11).

The index rose with clustering at every step of the graded orders (Table 2, Figure 2). The registered test, a linear trend in the natural log of B, was supported (slope 1.63 nats per log unit, SE 0.10, Holm p $<$~.001; seed level, 1.63 [1.46,~1.80]). The rise is not linear in log B, however. It was about 0.6 to 1.0 nats per log unit up to B = 7, and 2.7 and 4.6 nats per log unit over the two highest steps. Above B = 3.6 it was close to linear in B itself, about 0.2 to 0.3 nats per unit. Already at B = 3.6 the index was higher than in random order (1.43 against 0.47; difference 0.96 [0.63,~1.28], Holm p $<$~.001), in all 15 seed-by-size pairs. The Objects corpora repeated this with different-object runs at the same level (1.18 against 0.22 for their own random order; difference 0.96 [0.37,~1.54], Holm p $<$~.001).

The corpora themselves suggest why. For every pair of consecutive sentences we counted how often the second repeats the first one's shape or colour (exploratory; Table E3). A learner that predicted each feature from the previous sentence alone, ignoring the noun, would show an index from \textminus{}3.26 (grouped Colour stable) to 3.39 (grouped different-object runs). Across the 12 conditions of Table E3 the model index tracked this reference closely (Pearson r = .95; exploratory and descriptive, since conditions share seeds). The model index sums over the whole continuation and the reference concerns one feature word, so only their agreement across conditions is comparable. The one clear departure was grouped same-object runs (2.99 against 0.37), where the models repeated shape, which every frame names, more than the corpus warrants. Arrangement thus changes the local statistics available when each sentence is predicted, and the models' index followed them.

\subsubsection*{What kind of grouping helps}
Under our matched-tokens recipe, a longer window did not raise the index (Section 6 discusses the confound with batch makeup). With 512-token windows, which hold about four times as many sentences, random-order Regular models gave 0.50 nats against 0.47 with 128 tokens (difference 0.03 [\textminus{}0.44,~0.50], Holm p = .87), and the registered equivalence test did not show equivalence either (p = .15). At B = 3.6, the longer window gave less, 0.80 against 1.43 (difference \textminus{}0.63 [\textminus{}0.90,~\textminus{}0.37]; two-sided seed-level p = .003). With the longer window, Regular models were still above No stable feature models (0.50 against 0.10, Holm p = .045; seed level, 4 of 5 seeds, p = .032).

At matched clustering, runs of different objects produced a larger index than runs re-describing one object. In grouped order, different-object runs gave 5.65 nats and same-object runs 2.99 (difference 2.66 [1.57,~3.75], Holm p $<$~.001; higher in all 15 seed-by-size pairs). At B = 3.6, different-object runs gave 1.18 and same-object runs 0.45 (difference 0.73 [0.15,~1.32], Holm p $<$~.001; 13 of 15). Same-object runs at this level were neither reliably different from random order (0.45 against 0.22; difference 0.22 [\textminus{}0.30,~0.74]) nor equivalent to it (registered secondary test).

The components show how the two kinds of run differ (Figure 3). After different-object runs, models expected shape to repeat and colour to change ($\Delta$ colour \textminus{}0.28 in grouped order). After same-object runs they expected colour to repeat as well (1.65), because in those runs every feature does repeat. Their index is still positive because they repeated shape, named in all four object frames, more than colour, named in two. Part of this may be copying.

\subsubsection*{Is the expectation tied to the noun?\nohdpunct}
On novel nouns the index was the same after the same noun and after a different noun. In random-order Regular models the difference was 0.005 nats (registered test; equivalent to zero within $\pm$0.2, p = .001), and in grouped Regular models, with an index of 6.49, it was \textminus{}0.05 nats (seed level, 90\% interval [\textminus{}0.10,~0.01]; equivalent to zero, p = .002). At the seed level it was equivalent to zero in 25 of the 26 Study 1 conditions (exploratory; Appendix E). This does not show that the expectation ignores the noun, because the models showed almost no sign of tracking novel nouns. After grouped same-object runs, the models expected the previous object's colour after the same trained noun but not after a different one ($\Delta$ colour 1.14 against \textminus{}0.14; noun-specific index \textminus{}1.06, 0 of 15 models above zero; Appendix F), but on novel nouns the same models gave 0.04 (seed level, 90\% interval [\textminus{}0.09,~0.18]). Nor did any of the 60 models we checked predict a novel noun again after reading it (exploratory; Appendix E). The lost embedding tie (Sections 3.2 and 3.3) may be part of the reason.

Nouns from training give the informative test (a registered secondary analysis; Appendix F). The index on them was smaller than on novel nouns, because the shape in the context, drawn at random, conflicts with the shape the model has learned for that noun. In grouped Regular models it was 1.03 nats after the same noun and 0.73 after a different noun (noun-specific index 0.30, one-sided p = .008, uncorrected; 10 of 15 models above zero). In every condition with an index above 0.3 nats, most of it remained after a different noun (Table F1 and the repository).

The corpora gave strong evidence for a stronger dependence. In the grouped Regular corpora, a sentence about the same kind repeated the previous sentence's shape every time, and a sentence about a different kind did so 7\% of the time (Table E3). A learner that predicted the next shape from the previous sentence and the noun would show a noun-specific index of more than 10 nats. The models learned mainly the noun-blind part of this statistic. One reason may be that every trained kind keeps one shape, so the shape in a sentence about a trained kind can be predicted from the noun itself, without comparing it with the previous sentence. Same-object runs fit this account. There the colour of the next object cannot be predicted from its noun, and the models did tie their expectation about colour to the noun. Our experiments do not test the account directly.

\subsubsection*{Summary}
Arrangement taught the models which feature persists. Their index followed the local statistic each arrangement provides, but mostly not its dependence on the noun, and the registered test with novel nouns was uninformative.

\section{Study 2: How clustered is natural input?}
Study 2 measures, with the statistic B of Study 1, how clustered mentions of object kinds are in three kinds of natural input: speech recorded around young children, conversation between adults, and the written text language models are trained on. It is descriptive. B is a shared statistic, not a calibrated dose: natural corpora differ from the Study 1 corpora in many ways besides clustering.

\subsection{Method}
\textbf{Corpora.} We used the North American English collection of CHILDES \cite{macwhinney2000}, downloaded in full from TalkBank, kept every transcript whose target child was under 36 months, and took every utterance not spoken by that child. Most of this speech is addressed to the child, but some is addressed to others present; for brevity we call it speech to children. For adult conversation we used CABank: the Santa Barbara Corpus of Spoken American English \cite{dubois2004}, CallHome English \cite{canavan1997} and CallFriend English \cite{canavan1996a,canavan1996b}. All but the Santa Barbara Corpus are telephone calls, in which the speakers share no objects in view. For written text we used the first 10,000 documents of the Pile \cite{gao2020}, the training data of the Pythia models in Study 3, split into sentences. Documents of fewer than 400 words or with no mention of a target noun were excluded, leaving 2,116 transcripts from 35 CHILDES corpora, 245 CABank transcripts from four sources and 2,474 Pile documents from 21 subsets.

\textbf{Kinds.} We counted mentions of 70 count nouns from the MacArthur-Bates Communicative Development Inventory \cite{fenson2007} that name solid, inanimate objects, such as \emph{ball}, \emph{cup}, \emph{shoe} and \emph{truck}, the kinds for which children show the shape bias. Singular and plural count as one kind, and words often used as verbs or adjectives in speech to children, such as \emph{brush} and \emph{orange}, were left out. Because the nouns come from a list of words young children know, they are more frequent in speech to children than in the other corpora, by design.

\textbf{Measure.} B was computed as in Study 1, with the utterances or sentences of each document shuffled for $P_0$, within a window of 100 words, about the length of the 128-token training window of Study 1. Intervals resample sources (CHILDES corpora, CABank sources or Pile subsets) and then documents within them.

\textbf{What repeated labels refer to.} B counts repeated labels. It cannot tell two balls from one ball named twice, and Study 1 showed that the difference matters. The author coded 500 random same-label pairs (300 from speech to children, 100 from each of the others), with source labels withheld, as referring to the same object, to different objects of the kind, to the kind in general, or unclear; source could sometimes be guessed from the text. As a model-assisted consistency check, an instance of Claude (Anthropic), given only the coding rules and the pairs, coded a random quarter of them without access to the first coder's codes; the prompt and the codes are archived. Agreement on 124 pairs was 84\% (Cohen's $\kappa$ = 0.78). This is not a measure of agreement between human coders.

\textbf{Effective clustering (exploratory).} As a rough projection onto the Study 1 scale, we discounted each corpus's excess clustering by the share of coded pairs that referred to different objects, d, out of those that referred to particular objects: $B_{\mathrm{eff}}$ = 1 + (B \textminus{} 1)d. A strict version takes d out of all coded pairs. Intervals resample sources and documents for B and coded pairs for d. $B_{\mathrm{eff}}$ assumes that only different-object pairs inform the learner. Study 1 shows that same-object repetition is not neutral, so $B_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is a heuristic, not a corrected estimate.

\subsection{Results}
Object labels were clustered in every corpus, most of all in speech to children (Table 3). In speech to children B was well above 1 (registered test, Holm p $<$~.001), above written text (difference 2.14 [1.55,~2.91], Holm p $<$~.001) and above adult conversation (difference 1.24 [0.51,~2.03]; registered two-sided test, p $<$~.001). The order of the three held in three registered secondary analyses: when each transcript was weighted equally, when every common noun was counted instead of our 70, and when documents were shuffled only within 500-word blocks, which keeps changes of topic. That last check lowered B in speech to children from 3.57 to 2.08, so part of the raw clustering reflects topic.

Nearly half of the repeated labels in speech to children referred again to the same object, and about a quarter brought a different object, a share similar to adult conversation. Most repeated labels in written text did not refer to a physical object, or referred to the kind in general. Effective clustering of different objects was therefore lower than B in every corpus. Speech to children was above written text on this measure (difference 0.73 [0.41,~1.09]) but not reliably above adult conversation (0.29 [\textminus{}0.14,~0.69]).

Placed on the Study 1 scale by effective clustering, as Amendment 7 specified, written text lies between random order and the graded order at B = 1.4, and adult conversation and speech to children lie between the graded orders at B = 1.4 and B = 3.6 (Figure 2). Over that range the index of the small models rose only from 0.47 to 1.43 nats. This does not say what a model trained on each corpus would learn.

The corpora differ more in how often such repetitions occur. Speech to children contained 10.1 same-label pairs per 1,000 words, against about 1 in the other two. Combining these rates with the coded shares, it contained about 2.8 nearby repetitions per 1,000 words that bring a different object of a kind, against about 0.2 in adult conversation and 0.1 in written text (exploratory). Part of this difference comes from our choice of nouns. B is a ratio and does not capture density, which Study 1 held fixed.

\section{Study 3: Pretrained models}
Study 3 asks whether models trained on large written corpora show the expectation, how it depends on size and training, and whether they tie it to the noun. It is a descriptive comparison: the arrangement of their training data is not manipulated.

\subsection{Method}
\textbf{Items.} The items follow the format of Study 1 in English. Each feature gets its own sentence, so English adjective order cannot favour one dimension, and the order of the three sentences is counterbalanced:

\begin{quote}
Context: \emph{Here is a snerg. It is cylindrical. It is purple. It is smooth.} Repeat: \emph{Here is another snerg. It is \textbf{cylindrical}. It is turquoise. It is fluffy.} New: \emph{Here is another snerg. It is \textbf{circular}. It is turquoise. It is fluffy.}
\end{quote}

Each dimension uses eight English words of similar frequency, and the nouns are novel words spelled with ordinary subword pieces (Appendix G). In the original items the same-noun continuation began \emph{Here is another snerg} and the different-noun continuation \emph{Here is a zull}, so the noun-specific index compared the noun and the word \emph{another} together. A later registration added four item sets with 16 nouns, in which the two continuations share their wording: \emph{a} in both (the registered primary set), \emph{another} in both, a second frame, and a second word list.

\textbf{Models.} The Pythia models \cite{biderman2023,vanderwal2025} range from 14 million to 12 billion parameters and were trained on the same 300 billion tokens of the Pile in the same order. We scored their final checkpoints and checkpoints across training, and nine further training seeds of the five smallest sizes \cite{vanderwal2025}. We also scored the two GPT-2 baselines of the BabyLM Challenge (124 million parameters, trained on 10 and 100 million words; \citealp{charpentier2025}), and Qwen2.5 72B \cite{qwen2024} and Llama 3.1 70B \cite{grattafiori2024} as descriptive reference points. Appendix G lists every checkpoint.

\textbf{Tests.} Trends with size are Spearman correlations across Pythia sizes; trends over training are tested by permuting checkpoints within size; intervals resample nouns. The pooled noun-specific test uses a mixed model with crossed random effects for nouns, items and feature words.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{The index rose with size but not steadily over training.} At the end of training the shape-over-colour index rose with Pythia size (Spearman $\rho$ = .92, Holm p = .003), from \textminus{}2.44 nats at 14 million parameters to 1.17 at 12 billion (Figure 4). This raw index includes effects of the particular words. Untrained Pythia models give about 0.5 nats, because in some colour pairs the repeated and the new word take different numbers of tokens, and an untrained model pays roughly the same cost for every token. On the original items whose two words take the same number of tokens (all shape pairs and a quarter of the colour pairs; exploratory), untrained models gave \textminus{}0.32 to 0.31 nats, the trend with size held ($\rho$ = .93), and every Pythia model from 1B upwards gave an index above zero, from 1.00 [0.04,~1.82] at 1B to 1.62 [0.86,~2.19] at 12B. The trend with size held on every item set, including the second word list ($\rho$ = .83 to .95; exploratory). The index did not rise steadily over training (mean within-size $\rho$ = \textminus{}.34). At every size, training first lowered it, to about \textminus{}2 nats between steps 1,000 and 4,000, much as in the random-order models of Study 1, and only the larger models recovered. The BabyLM baselines did not show a reliable index (10 million words, 0.47 [\textminus{}1.05,~1.93]; 100 million words, 0.37 [\textminus{}0.04,~0.73]; Holm p = .52 and .12). Pythia models of similar size did not show it either, even after 300 billion tokens, but the two families differ in more than their data, so this comparison does not separate data from size.

\textbf{Weak, wording-dependent evidence of noun binding.} On the original items the noun-specific index was positive in every Pythia model from 410 million parameters, but those items confounded the noun with \emph{another}. With \emph{a} in both continuations, the registered tests were not supported: the index did not rise with size ($\rho$ = \textminus{}.42, Holm p = 1.00), and pooled over Pythia 410M to 12B it was not above zero (\textminus{}0.15 nats, SE 0.12, Holm p = 1.00). It was higher at 410M than at 160M across ten training seeds of each (0.15 against \textminus{}0.15 nats, Holm p = .012), with 7 of the 10 seeds at 410M positive. With \emph{another} in both continuations (a registered secondary analysis), it was above zero in four of the six Pythia models from 410M (0.28, 0.77, 0.30 and 0.17 nats at 410M, 1B, 2.8B and 6.9B), but not at 1.4B or 12B, and it was small and mixed in sign on the second frame and the second word list. Of the two reference models of about 70 billion parameters, Qwen2.5 gave a positive index with \emph{a} in both continuations (0.56 [0.28,~0.84]) and Llama 3.1 did not (\textminus{}0.16 [\textminus{}0.36,~0.06]).

The components show why the wording matters. After \emph{another}, which marks a new member of the kind, the larger models expected shape to repeat and colour to change more after the same noun than after a different one, and \emph{another} also raised the raw index, to 0.49 to 1.08 nats in Pythia 1B to 12B (\textminus{}0.34 to 0.46 with a in both). After \emph{a}, the same noun may name the same object again, and then every feature should repeat: colour repeated more after the same noun, and the noun-specific index fell.

\textbf{Texture and context.} No Pythia, Qwen or Llama model preferred shape over texture, so what the large models show is a shape-over-colour expectation, not a general shape bias (image classifiers trained on ImageNet likewise favour texture over shape; \citealp{geirhos2019}). The BabyLM baseline trained on 10 million words did prefer shape over texture (1.17 [0.24,~2.34]). The two largest Pythia models tied texture to the noun more than shape. When two or four exemplars in the context shared a shape, every trained model followed the context, strongly so from 410 million parameters. What developed with size was the expectation after one exemplar, not the use of evidence in the context.

\textbf{What was learned.} Grouping by kind taught small transformers which feature carries over from one object description to the next, and their index followed the local statistic each arrangement provides. A likely route lies in the training objective. In grouped order the next sentence is often about another member of the same kind; its fixed feature can be predicted from the sentence before and its varying features cannot, so grouping raises the predictive value of carrying the fixed feature forward. In random order the previous sentence is about the same kind only about 3\% of the time. The models tied this to the noun only in small part, although the grouped corpora made that dependence strong. They used a trained noun where the noun alone could not predict the feature, as for colour after same-object runs; in the Regular corpora the noun alone predicts the fixed shape, so comparing nouns adds little. Our experiments identify the training conditions that produce the behaviour, not the mechanism inside the models. Carrying a word forward from the previous sentence is the kind of behaviour attributed to induction heads \cite{olsson2022}, but we did not examine attention.

\textbf{The longer window.} At B = 3.6 the longer window gave a lower index. We did not predict this and have no tested account. With tokens per step held fixed, the 512-token models saw a quarter as many sequences per step (Table C1), with a different distribution of positions, so the result applies to this recipe, not to window length alone.

\textbf{Same-object repetition.} When the next sentence describes the same object, every feature repeats, and the right prediction is to copy all of them. Same-object runs taught the models to expect colour to repeat as well, although they repeated shape more than the corpus warrants, and on nouns from training they expected the previous object's colour after the same noun. Pythia models showed a parallel contrast: after \emph{another}, the larger models expected shape to repeat and colour to change more after the same noun than after a different one, and after \emph{a}, colour repeated more after the same noun. Repetition teaches selectively which feature persists when it brings different objects of a kind; re-describing one object teaches that every feature persists. How often a kind recurs is not enough; what recurs matters.

\textbf{Natural input.} Speech to children was more clustered than written text, but nearly half of its nearby repetitions returned to the same object, so its effective clustering of different objects was only modestly higher. It differed more in density, partly because of our choice of nouns. Study 1 held density fixed, so we do not know how much density adds.

\textbf{Training data.} Arrangement can be changed without changing content. Beyond packing related documents into one window \cite{shi2024,zhao2024} or ordering by difficulty \cite{diehlmartinez2023}, our results suggest a specific aim for regularities that hold across items: place different instances of a kind near each other, rather than repeated descriptions of one instance. They also suggest a limit, which we did not test directly. Even when the data make persistence depend on the kind, a learner that can learn each kind's fixed feature directly need not learn to compare kinds. A direct test of the noun-tied form would train on kinds that cannot be learned one by one, such as many new kinds each seen only briefly, as in meta-learning approaches to word learning \cite{wang2025}.

\textbf{What the results do not show.} They concern a next-word learner trained on text. Children also see, handle and remember objects, and spacing presentations apart can help their category learning \cite{vlach2008}, so the results do not show how children learn.

\section{Limitations}
The artificial language of Study 1 has 32 kinds, three dimensions, one fixed feature shared by every kind and five sentence frames. The frames name shape more often than colour or texture, and frame 4 states that a colour differs, which may add to the negative $\Delta$ for colour in the Regular corpora (\textminus{}1.13 in grouped order, against \textminus{}0.28 in the Objects corpora, which lack frame 4)\emph{}. Conclusions therefore rest on comparisons between corpora that share the frames, and the value of $\Delta$ for one dimension is not an estimate of a model's intrinsic preference among dimensions. A replication with the frames counterbalanced across dimensions would remove this asymmetry. The models are small and were trained with one recipe, with five seeds per condition. Frame 3 says another even when a same-object run re-describes the same object, and the test frame does not mark the second object as new, which may lower the noun-specific index on trained nouns. The models did not track the untrained test nouns, so the registered noun test was uninformative. A decisive test of the noun would train on corpora in which each kind's fixed shape is reassigned between passes, so that the previous sentence predicts the shape but the noun alone does not. The noun result rests on a registered secondary test with trained nouns (uncorrected), whose items set the context against the shape learned for each noun. Every row with a Holm-adjusted p below .001 stays below .05 when corrected across all 25 registered hypotheses. B counts repeated labels, not referents. Effective clustering rests on 500 pairs coded by one person, with a model as second coder, and is an unvalidated heuristic. Three of the four adult sources are telephone calls. All corpora are English. Study 3 does not manipulate the arrangement of pretraining data, so it cannot show that clustering causes the pattern it reports; a direct test would pretrain larger models on the same text in different arrangements.

\textbf{The loading correction.} The model code ties the input embedding to the output layer. \textbf{Noun-specific index.} At the seed level, the noun-specific index was equivalent to zero (two one-sided tests, $\pm$0.2 nats) in 25 of the 26 Study 1 conditions; the exception was same-object runs at B = 3.6 (\textminus{}0.11, p = .058). No condition exceeded 0.15 nats in absolute value. These nulls are uninformative, because the models did not track novel nouns (Section 3.5). As a further check, for the 60 models in the random and grouped Regular and grouped Objects conditions we compared the log probability of the same novel noun after the context and the word A with the mean over the other seven novel nouns. The difference was no more than 0.033 nats in absolute value in every model, and positive on about half the items. Because the novel nouns were never training targets, this check also depends on output weights that training only pushed down, so it is weaker evidence than the trained-noun contrast in Section 3.5.

\textbf{Correction across all tests.} Holm correction was applied within each registered family of tests, as registered. Every row of Table A4 with a Holm-adjusted p below .001 has an unadjusted p below .001, and so stays below .05 under Holm correction across all 25 tests.

\textbf{What the corpora reward.} For every pair of consecutive sentences in each training corpus (five seeds pooled), Table E3 gives the share about the same kind and how often the second sentence repeats the first one's shape or colour. The noun-blind reference index is D(shape) \textminus{} D(colour), where D = ln p \textminus{} ln((1 \textminus{} p)/9) and p is the share of pairs that repeat that feature, with ten values per dimension; it is what a learner that predicts each feature from the previous sentence alone would show on a one-exemplar item. The noun-aware reference computes the same index separately for pairs about the same kind and pairs about different kinds (add-0.5 smoothing).

\textbf{Object nouns (Study 2).} ball, balloon, block, book, crayon, doll, puzzle, toy; airplane, bicycle, boat, bus, car, motorcycle, stroller, tractor, train, truck; apple, banana, carrot, cookie, cracker, egg, grape, raisin, sandwich; boot, button, coat, diaper, hat, jacket, mitten, necklace, pajamas, pants, shirt, shoe, sock, sweater, zipper; basket, blanket, bottle, bowl, box, broom, clock, cup, dish, fork, hammer, key, lamp, pencil, pillow, plate, purse, scissors, spoon, telephone, toothbrush, towel; bed, chair, couch, crib, table, television.

\textbf{Coding rules (Study 2).} Each pair was shown with the utterance before it, the stretch between the two mentions and the utterance after, without its source. \emph{Same}: both mentions refer to the same physical object. \emph{Different}: two or more distinct objects of the kind (another, other, numbers, plurals that count objects, descriptions that cannot fit one object). \emph{Generic}: at least one mention refers to the kind in general. \emph{Unclear}: the words do not settle it, including labels not used for a physical object. The second coder coded a random 25\% of pairs (seed 2031). The full rubric and the second coder's prompt are in the repository.

\textbf{Feature words (Study 3).} Word list 1: shape \emph{pointed, oval, circular, curved, rectangular, triangular, spherical, cylindrical}; colour \emph{grey, purple, amber, violet, scarlet, crimson, maroon, turquoise}; texture \emph{smooth, shiny, sticky, fuzzy, fluffy, furry, glossy, silky}. Word list 2: shape \emph{oblong, hexagonal, conical, tapered, elliptical, tubular, cubic, rounded}; colour \emph{magenta, lilac, teal, beige, indigo, lavender, ivory, coral}; texture \emph{velvety, waxy, prickly, bumpy, gritty, coarse, slippery, rough}. Mean Zipf frequencies for list 1 are 3.75 (shape), 3.82 (colour) and 3.70 (texture).

\textbf{Nouns (Study 3).} Original items: snerg, twick, vulf, wazz, glorp, yeff, zull, kleb. Added sets: these and brell, sprock, fendle, plim, skeb, tulver, grisp, vosh.

\textbf{Item counts (Study 3).} Original items: 576 one-exemplar items (8 nouns, 12 draws, 3 dimensions, 2 continuation nouns) and 2,304 with two or four exemplars sharing either shape or colour, 2,880 in all. Each added set has 1,152 one-exemplar items.

\textbf{Checkpoints (Study 3).} Pythia 14M, 70M, 160M, 410M, 1B, 1.4B and 2.8B at steps 0, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1,000, 4,000, 16,000, 64,000 and 143,000 (2.8B without step 64,000); Pythia 6.9B and 12B at steps 64, 1,000, 4,000, 8,000, 12,000, 16,000, 32,000 and 143,000; Pythia 410M to 2.8B also at every 1,000 steps from 2,000 to 15,000 and at 18,000, 20,000, 24,000 and 32,000; nine further training seeds of Pythia 14M, 31M, 70M, 160M and 410M (and the original 31M model) at steps 4,000, 16,000, 64,000 and 143,000; the BabyLM GPT-2 baselines and Qwen2.5 72B and Llama 3.1 70B at their released weights.
